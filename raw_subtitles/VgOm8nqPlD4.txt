A few years ago,
I was speaking with a business leader
who was frustrated.
He was always pitching
new ideas to his team
on how they could work
together better and faster.
But his team didn't seem
to take up those ideas very often.
At the end of our conversation,
he said something that stayed with me.
"I don't understand
why they won't just change."
In my work as a consultant,
I've watched people ask their colleagues
to change in a million different ways.
It's "I want you to change
the way you write that report,"
or "I want you to use these new inputs
in your demand forecast."
Or sometimes, "I want you
to meet with your manager more often."
They're different in detail,
but they're the same in spirit.
It's people asking other people
to do things differently.
Sometimes it works,
and sometimes it doesn’t.
But why?
Why do some asks succeed
when other asks fail?
What's the secret to getting
the people you work with to change?
After years of interacting
with business leaders,
behavioral scientists,
thousands of employees,
here's what I found.
There are two types of people
who want their coworkers to change,
and I call them the dreamers
and the detectives.
The dreamers mostly ask one question:
Why wouldn’t people do it?
Dreamers have a passionate belief
that the new way
of doing things is smarter,
faster, fairer than the old way.
They think the benefits are self-evident,
and they struggle to comprehend
that anyone else would see it differently.
But often, the dreamers
don't get their wish.
People don't change
because there are too many
barriers in their way.
The detectives ask a different question.
Not “Why wouldn’t people do it?”
but instead, “Why won’t people do it?”
Detectives don't get caught up
in their own feelings
about why the new way of doing things
is good or important.
Instead, they investigate the frictions,
the disincentives that will prevent
people from changing.
And consistently, it's the detectives
who get better results.
Here's one example.
A hotel chain wanted its staff members
to use a more modern,
sophisticated tool to set room prices,
one that relied more on automation
and machine learning.
But through good investigation,
leaders learned that staff
members were skeptical.
They weren't convinced
that the new tool could improve
on their own pricing instincts.
So they pulled together
the chain's most skilled,
experienced staff members
and put them into an early
user group for the new tool
to signal to the organization
it really was valuable.
And it worked.
Before long, the vast majority
of staff members
who had access to the tool
were using it every week.
In your own life,
I'm sure there are things
that you want other people
to do differently.
As a detective,
you can ask, “Why won’t they do it?”
in a few different ways.
Here's a simple one.
What knowledge and skills
are people missing?
This question roots out
gaps in the information
people have about the change
and gaps in their own ability
to play their part.
If this is the barrier,
you need to give people the information
and tools that they need.
Here's another variant.
What frictions will get in people's way?
Sometimes people feel
they don't have the time
or resources to do things differently.
Sometimes other people
are quietly encouraging them
not to change at all.
In these cases, you have
to remove the frictions,
provide time, provide resources,
have hard conversations
with those other influential people.
One more for you to consider.
Why won't people want to change?
When people feel they don't gain
anything by changing,
or worse,
when they feel that they
have something to lose,
we shouldn't be surprised
when they don't change at all.
When this happens, you have to help
people see things differently,
alleviate their unfounded fears
or show how you will personally help
prevent the worst of them.
When you ask these questions,
you learn a lot.
You learn that the person you're asking
to run the new risk assessment
is the very same person
who designed the old one.
To them, the change
represents a loss of status.
You learn that your operations manager
loves scheduling truck deliveries.
To them, the change to an automated system
represents the loss
of a treasured creative activity.
And once you've learned, you can act.
As you get started,
here are a few tips to keep in mind.
First, ask out loud.
It's good to imagine
why people won't change,
but it's much better to sit them down
in a meeting or over coffee
and actually ask them.
Second, ask more than once.
Often you won't find
the complex barriers to change,
like deep personal fears,
until you've addressed the simple ones,
like not having enough time.
Finally, never moralize.
When people aren't behaving
the way you expect,
it's tempting to say
that they're lazy or resistant,
but moralizing doesn’t help you
get the thing that you want:
a successful, sustained change.
By the way, you can be a detective
outside the workplace as well.
Maybe you want your sister to start
using the family's shared calendar,
or an old friend to send
more life updates.
The same principles apply.
So the next time you want someone
to do something differently,
remember the magic question.
Don’t ask, “Why wouldn’t they do it?”
Ask instead, “Why won’t they do it?”
You'll be surprised
how changing the question
can change everything.
Thank you.
