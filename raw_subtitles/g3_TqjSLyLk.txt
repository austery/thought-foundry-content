Does anyone else feel,
no matter what you think about AI,
[that] it just feels a little bit wrong?
You use it,
you're afraid you're cutting corners.
You don’t use it,

You're optimistic, you get side-eye.
Everything is just a little bit off.
I've been advising executives
and board leaders

Sitting in room after room,
company after company,
I can tell you, it's not just you.
This confusing feeling --

AI is already redesigning work.
Leadership teams --

spinning up too much change all at once
in an uncoordinated way,
or letting all of us do individual DIY

This person-by-person job redesign

Let me give you an example.
One well-seasoned manager

"My team is producing 10x more output,
10x faster,

This manager felt she was more lonely --

The output sometimes was better,

I talked to lots of managers.
It wasn't just this one manager.
Lots of people feel this way.
My key message to you:
AI will dramatically reshape more

So this was never a question

This was always a question of how:
how we lead through that redesign.
I'm going to take you

leadership in the redesign.
I'll walk through some examples

attention,

First, attention.
My colleagues and I have been studying
the experience of working with AI.
One senior engineering manager said,
"It's like having a dozen
browser tabs open in my head,
all fighting for attention.
I was working harder to manage the tools
than to solve the problem."
This is brain fry.
Not burnout -- something different.
It results from the acute
oversight of AI tools.
It’s mental fog, slower
decision-making, more errors.
Workers who experience brain fry
report 39 percent more major errors.
They are also 39 percent more likely
to intend to leave.
Your most active AI users
are at risk of walking out.
No one set out to create brain fry.
It happened because we piled
tool on top of tool
on existing workflows
without removing work
or thinking about it as a team.
The work we added was often
cognitively more demanding.
So what do we do about it?
We need to redesign work and workflows
as teams with outcomes in mind.
We need to engage managers.
I know managers already
have a lot on their plate,
but when you have managers answering
questions about AI for employees,
mental fatigue scores
are 15 percent lower.
One company thought through this
and started to redesign job descriptions,
naming the more strategic work.
They took out handoffs
that were no longer needed.
They created a broad upskilling plan.
All of this was done in concert --
not individually one by one.
Second, accountability.
Companies are putting
AI agents on org charts.
They're giving them names, job titles.
We were curious.
We ran an experiment:
same document, same errors.
Only difference:
who we said the drafter was.
In one group, an AI tool --
in one group, an AI “employee” --
in the third group, an actual employee.
For managers who were already
familiar with the concept
of AI agents on org charts,
personal accountability fell
by nine percentage points
when they reviewed work
done by an AI "employee."
They found 18 percent fewer errors.
Escalations were up by 44 percent.
Effectively, they
scrutinized the work less,
found fewer errors
and gave their job to someone
else more frequently.
One company in our study
even named their AI.
Let's just call it Bob.
When mistakes happened,
Bob made the mistake.
This has a real organizational cost.
Leaders, we didn't mean
to rewire accountability.
We thought putting AI agents on org charts
would make it more familiar.
That small choice impacted
the amount of accountability
that was felt by those reviewing work.
To overcome this, agentic systems
need a human overseer.
We need a person
with clear decision rights
and [an] escalation path
and a career consequence
if something goes wrong.
We also need training for employees
on how to review AI content.
Do this before you name your AI.
Third, apprenticeship.
This is the one I think the most about.
The entry-level work,
the drafting, analysis,
first cuts, research,
increasingly, that can be done
by AI, at least somewhat.
This work was how people learned.
It was the at-bats that we all needed
early in our careers.
Our research shows when juniors use AI

they're able to produce good output.
However, without expert support,

It's output without learning.
I get asked this question every week

How do I build

Companies that are tackling this

more project-based.
They're pairing juniors

They're even leaning on the natural

Even more importantly,
they're taking the time to rewrite
manager and leadership competencies.
They're including coaching,
upskilling on AI, rewiring work as a team.
Perhaps this is our opportunity
to invest in the development-focused
managers we've always said we wanted.
Attention, accountability, apprenticeship.
These are three areas
where the work redesign is happening to us
instead of being led by us --
if we're not careful.
The core work of a leader right now
is to optimize this work
with good outcomes in mind
and the best of human
and machine intelligence.
Companies that are getting

Those conflicting feelings

We need leaders to name what's changing.
