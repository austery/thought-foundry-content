Hello everyone, I’m Ma Yujiang, and I   work in art. What exactly is art? I   don’t think anyone can explain it   clearly, so today I won’t talk about   art directly. Instead, I’ll share   four stories from my life from a   different perspective. My mother passed   away when I was three years old. From   then on, I lived with my grandparents   in the countryside, in the house you   see in this picture. One day, I was   playing outside with my cousin. My   grandfather came back from the fields   and said, "Your mom is home." I was so   happy at that moment. I jumped up and   down, shouting, "My mom is back! My mom   is back!" Then my cousin said, "  That’s my mom, not yours. Your mom is   dead." In that instant, I felt like I   finally understood what death meant.   From that point on, I often used the   charcoal left over from my   grandmother’s cooking to draw myself   and my mother on this section of the   concrete wall. Sometimes, when I   finished drawing, the rain would wash   it away. So I would draw it again. If   the rain washed it away again, I would   draw it again. It felt like I was   defying fate; Heaven took my mother   away, but I refused to accept it. Since   then, the belief that I must draw   myself and my mother together became   like a seed planted in my heart. When I   was twelve, I found a photo in my   father’s desk drawer one day. I   didn't know who it was, so I took it to   ask my grandmother. Pointing to the   child, she said, "This is you, and the   person next to you is your mother."   That was the first time I ever saw a   picture of me and my mother together.   When my grandfather returned from the   fields at noon, he told me more details   about the photo. The photo was taken in   May 1989; I was one year old, and my   mother was 27. With this photo, the   seed that had been in my heart since I   was three finally began to sprout. I   naturally embarked on the path of   painting, and I was very fortunate to   be admitted to the Central Academy of   Fine Arts. By my junior year of college  , I felt my skills were good enough, so   I painted my first portrait of myself   and my mother together. I was 21, and   my mother was 27, just as she looked in   the photo. I painted this in 2009. You   can see the character "Pan" in the   lower-left corner, which was the   nickname my mother gave me. After she   passed away, no one called me by that   name again. Since I turned 21, I’ve   painted one portrait every year.   Paintings of me with my mother; I call   this series "Mother Tongue." I usually   choose significant life events as the   background for these paintings. Like   this one at 22 for college graduation,   25 when I moved to Hong Kong, and 27   when I returned home. My grandparents   were getting old and couldn't handle   the corn harvest, so I went back to   help. This is me just returning from   the fields, exhausted—that’s this   painting here. My mother and I are the   same age. We are both 27, and at 30, I   was in New York with the Hudson River   as the background. At 32, it was the   pandemic; I wasn't vaccinated and   couldn't go anywhere. Life moved a bit   slower. I went to the beach, just   drinking beer by the shore. The second   year of the pandemic, I went to the   mountains often, so I painted this one   with the mountains in the back. Those   three years of the pandemic felt frozen   in time, so I traveled back to age 1   and painted myself from 1 to 20 with my   mother. This is age 1; naturally, I   used the only photo I had of my mother   and me for it. Two years old. You can   see here in the corner that this photo   was taken at a studio. Growing up in   the countryside as a post-80s kid, not   everyone had photos; by chance, my   stepmother ran a studio, so I had   photos every year—destiny is a truly   magical thing. Five, seven, eight, nine  , ten, eleven, twelve; twelve was   elementary school graduation, thirteen.   Fifteen, middle school graduation,   sixteen, seventeen. Nineteen, off to   college, twenty. After the pandemic   ended, everything returned to normal;   at 35, the background is Victoria   Harbour. Sometimes when I’m painting   my mother, I suddenly remember certain   details; for instance, when painting   her profile, I’d suddenly think, "Oh.  ""So that's how her nose looked from   the side." That’s why for this one, I   painted a half-body portrait; with a   larger scale, I could capture her face   in more detail. While working on other   pieces, I might recall, "Oh, her   cheekbone actually looked like this."   When transforming a blank page bit by   bit, it actually stirs up fond memories   for me. Thinking about it out of thin   air, I probably wouldn't recall as much  . Last year I was 37, ten years older   than my mother. That’s the scene I   was painting. This year I'm 38, and I   haven't painted it yet; I usually do it   toward the end of the year. I plan to   hold an exhibition when I turn 40,   putting these paintings together, and   do it again at 50, 60, and 70. So, this   work will likely be at its best when   I’m 80 or 90 years old. Of course,   that’s assuming I’m still alive. By   then, there will be a gray-haired son   with a cane or in a wheelchair, with a   27-year-old mother beside him. So, in   this series, I am not just myself; I am   also a part of the artwork. All I need   to do is live well. For every year I   live, the work gains another piece.   This work seems to have a life of its   own. When I pass away one day, it   won’t just be the death of me as an   individual, but also the proof that   this work is complete. As you can see,   in this work, I encountered a very   significant dilemma: the death of my   mother. Usually, such major dilemmas   inspire my creativity. I love this way   of having an artwork grow out of one's   own life. I think there is a Chinese   idiom for art that I find very fitting:   "expressing what one feels." These four   characters capture the essence. It’s   not just about art history or following   trends; you just look inward. Whatever   moves you, that is what you should   create. However, life isn't always   filled with such major dilemmas;   sometimes, smaller challenges also   trigger my reflection. Next, I’d like   to tell you about this work, "Letters   from Hong Kong." In 2014, I went to   Hong Kong. Back then, I didn't have an   ID card, couldn't work, had no friends,   didn't understand the language, and   couldn't adapt to the climate. My   husband is a Hong Kong local. He was   just working his regular job. I felt   extremely stifled staying at home. I   wanted to go out for a walk, but at the   time, I didn't have a local SIM card. I   was afraid he wouldn't find me when he   got home, so I wrote a letter of about   200 characters. The meaning was that I   had been in Hong Kong for over a month   by then. I always wanted to create   something, but I hadn't been able to   produce anything. And once I went out,   I was worried I would get lost. Every   time I reached a street sign, I would   take a photo of it; for example, when I   walked to Queen's Road, I took a   picture of Queen's Road. When I walked   to Des Voeux Road, I took a picture of   Des Voeux Road. If I got lost, I would   follow the signs back home, and when I   reached Po Yuen Path, I realized the   first character "Po" was the same as   the "Po" in "baby" from the letter. So   I thought, why not try to find every   single character from the letter and   see if it's possible, and that’s how   this project began. Here, I’d like to   show you the first few sentences. "Baby  , I've been in Hong Kong for over a   month now." This is the first sentence   of the letter. As you can see, I’m   using the technique of frottage; why   use this method? Why not use   photography? Because frottage requires   my body to be pressed firmly against   the wall. It’s like caressing the   city, which really mirrors my state of   mind at the time. But with photography,   I would have to distance myself from   the subject, and I feel that frottage   is perfectly suited for this work. Let   me show you the second sentence: "  During this time, I always want to do   something; otherwise, I feel that   staying home every day is meaningless."   These are the first three sentences of   the letter, which has over 200   characters in total; I’m still   working on it, and finding the   remaining characters will be harder.   One memory that stands out is the "  bottom" character under the bridge; I   went there at 3 a.m. with a ladder and   had been rubbing for half an hour   without finishing. I was almost done,   but I didn't expect them to restock at   4 a.m., so the employee came to open   the shop at 3:30 a.m. and saw me on the   ladder. He asked, "Are you here to help   us change the sign?" I said, "Yes." He   didn't say anything and went upstairs;   ten minutes passed. I had already   finished. Once done, I came down, and   the employee came back down too, saying  , "I just called the boss, and they   didn't send anyone to change the sign.   What are you actually doing?" Realizing   I’d been caught, I immediately   grabbed the paper and ran. Another   memorable one was the Po Kok Buddhist   Temple. Hong Kong has very strict laws   for heritage conservation. The Po Kok   Buddhist Temple is a historic building,   so I was lucky; I had finished the   rubbing and stepped off the ladder when   the security guard finally spotted me,   came over to stop me, and said I had   violated the heritage protection law   and insisted on keeping the evidence of   my crime. That is that piece of paper.   I certainly wouldn't give it to him.   This character is not easy to find.   Because this "jue"(覺), as in feeling,   is very hard to find. I tried   everything, but they wouldn't agree. I   told them I'm a typeface designer who   specializes in fonts, and that their   character is very iconic, so I couldn't   let it go. In the end, while he was   calling the police, I climbed over a   wall and ran away. This character is "  na"(哪), as in "where." I looked for   it for several years but couldn't find   it. I later realized the reason why.   Why couldn't I find it? Because Hong   Kong doesn't use "na"(哪); it only   uses "na"(那). These two characters   are not distinguished there. However, I   felt I had to find the original   character. Until later, a shop called "  Na Organic" opened, and I said, "Hey, I   finally found it!" This is right from   their "Na Organic" sign. This piece of   work actually has a connection to some   of my childhood experiences. My family   was poor when I was a child. We had no   books and no TV. How did I learn to   read characters? During the snowy   winter at Chinese New Year, my   grandfather would take my hand, and we   would go to every house to hang spring   couplets. We'd walk up to the front   door, and my grandfather would point at   the characters— "Increase the years,   increase the life; fill the universe   with blessings"—and that’s how I   learned to read. It was a comprehensive  , multi-sensory perception. Even now,   when I think of the character "chun"(  穿) for "pierce," I always feel it in   the upper right corner. The shape, the   color—this direct way of engaging   with the world, rather than just   gaining knowledge from books, had a   huge impact on me. For example,   everyone thinks the Hang Seng Bank is   just a bank, right? But when you walk   to the actual site, you realize it's   four separate characters: "Hang,""Seng,  ""Yin," and "Hang." If that work   established my connection to the city   of Hong Kong...Then this next piece of   work established my connection to the   people of Hong Kong. I went to Hong   Kong in 2014, and I wasn't very   accustomed to it then; by 2015, I was   even less adapted. So, I often suffered   from insomnia. When I couldn't sleep   and felt hungry at night, I would go to   McDonald's. I discovered that many   homeless people were sleeping in   McDonald's. I noticed a very   interesting phenomenon. They would   actually buy things and consume; it   wasn't like they bought nothing at all.   But they didn't have much money and   couldn't buy much. They usually bought   just one item, like a Coke for a few   dollars. Their receipts were just this   long, whereas a normal one might have a   few more lines of text—and a few more   lines mean a longer receipt. I found   this quite interesting. I just wanted   to collect these receipts. But I didn't   have an ID, so I couldn't work. I   noticed that McDonald's employees '  uniforms are simple—just a black   T-shirt. I embroidered a...Well, I   think you've all guessed it; I bought a   black T-shirt, stitched an "M" with   yellow thread, and went to work. These   receipts aren't useful to the people   sleeping at McDonald's after midnight,   because there are no customers then,   and the staff won't kick them out.   I’d go over to help wipe the tables,   put the trash on a tray, and slip the   receipt into my pocket. In one year, I   collected 1,259 receipts. They fade   over time, so I scanned them   immediately and measured the weight of   each one. Since some receipts are short   with only one line of info, like a   small soda, they are very light.   Probably about 0.3 to 0.5 grams. But   normal ones with a soda, fries, burger,   and wings have more lines. They weigh   about 0.5 to 0.7 grams; this 0.2-gram   difference is like two different worlds   or social classes. I used a   high-precision scale, the kind used for   weighing diamonds. You can see this one   in the corner; these two are the   lightest of the lot. I was bored, so I   folded the receipt and tore off the   four corners, leaving it at 0.258 grams  . I wrote the weight and the time from   each receipt onto gauze. Why use gauze   as a material? Because it’s very   rough and inexpensive, yet it feels   incredibly vital, much like the   homeless people I encountered. In 2018,   I held an exhibition displaying over   400 pieces, which is one-third of the   entire series. This is how it looked on   display in Wan Chai, Hong Kong. If you   look closely, you’ll see these   receipts reflect shifts; more people go   to McDonald's when it rains, and also   during the winter. Their food choices   differ too; in summer, they prefer cold   items like ice cream or small sodas,   while in winter, they prefer hot food.   Hot chocolate pies or hot apple pies.   It’s like there’s a society within;   there are partitions, and the regulars   sleep in the dim, booth-like areas   where they can lie down. Those who come   here can only sit, as it's very bright   outside; they can sit but not lie down.   The regulars are mostly elderly, along   with some young Middle Eastern men and   elderly Southeast Asian women. Some of   them don't buy anything at all. But I   find it very interesting, so I use my   writing to document them. For instance,   one person here set up a large piece of   cardboard on the table. Maybe they were   afraid of the bright light, or perhaps   they didn't want to be seen, so I   couldn't even tell their gender.   There's another one who waits until   someone finishes their meal, then   immediately rushes over to sit down so   the staff thinks they’re a paying   customer and won’t kick them out. One   person was having a hamburger with such   a solemn, sacred air, but the lettuce   kept falling out. They opened the   burger box wide to increase the surface   area so the leaves would fall into the   box. Inside, they’d pick them up,   stuff them back into the burger, eat,   and when more fell, they’d stuff them   back in again. It felt as if they were   enjoying a grand feast. The most   heartbreaking moment for me this year   was on New Year's Eve, when a   white-haired old lady walked   tremblingly to the counter to grab a   tray, then walked over here to pick up   dozens of free sugar packets meant for   coffee. Her hunchback was severe; when   we pour things into our mouths, we just   tilt our heads, but she couldn't.   Because of her severe hunchback, she   had to crane her neck back as if it   were broken, moving very, very slowly  —lift, tilt, pour, lift, tilt, pour.   The whole process lasted over ten   minutes. With the pile of wrappers left   behind, it was truly heartbreaking. But   the most heartwarming moment of the   entire series happened just after the   Spring Festival. A young man who comes   often—he probably recognizes me as   we’ve made eye contact—handed me   his tray, and when I looked at the back   of it, he had written two lines.   Wishing you prosperity, and may you get   more luck. I felt so moved right then   and there. To think that someone living   such a difficult life was still sending   me well wishes. That "get more luck"   part meant wishing me an abundance of   good fortune. When I was working on   this piece, I would talk to some of the   curators in the industry, and they   would say, "Hey, if you think they're   so pitiful, why don't you just give   them a loaf of bread?""Ah, I don't   think that works. Why? Because I am in   the same group as these homeless people  ; if I were to step out and hand them   bread, that sense of balance would be   broken. I would become a benefactor and   they would be the recipients, so I   cannot pity them, I can only accompany   them, so that their sense of agency is   respected. Besides, on the other hand,   there are actually people who go to   distribute bread. Sometimes I would   sleep there, and wake up at   four-something in the morning. And I'd   be handed a piece of bread. There are   dedicated welfare organizations that   come to hand them bread, but this   question got me thinking. I feel that   the function of art isn't to change the   world directly, but to change it   indirectly. As an artist, if you make   your work well, you will move many   people, and those moved people will go   on to change the world directly, and   that impact is far, far greater than me   just buying a loaf of bread for them.   Next, I'd like to tell you about the   final piece," Expiration Date. "I went   to Hong Kong in 2014. And I received my   Hong Kong permanent residency in 2024.   All these years, I’ve been buying   groceries and cooking at home, just a   housewife. When I go grocery shopping,   I’ve developed a habit of checking   the expiration dates; the year I was   turning 30, I felt a bit emotional   about my first 30 years of life. I   naturally linked these expiration dates   to my own life’s timeline. At that   moment, I thought, for so many years   I've bought things to eat—fish when I   wanted fish, meat when I wanted meat—  everything was bought for utility, but   could I buy something useless instead?   It would be for the sake of beauty. But   this beauty isn't about looking good or   being pretty. It's about the beauty of   structure. I wondered if my life could   be more precise and more refined, so   how would that precision work? How   would that refinement work? I would   only eat things that expire on January   1st on January 1st, and only eat things   that expire on January 2nd on January   2nd. I wanted to keep this up for a   year. If I only eat those things, I   have to find them first, so I would   search for them, buy them if I found   them, and eat them once bought. If I   couldn't find them, I wouldn't have   anything to eat. In the end, I failed   during that year I turned 30. I only   found five items in the first month;   that didn't work, I was too hungry. In   the years that followed, I kept at it.   Plus, environmentalism became quite   popular and trendy back then, so   supermarkets would put a shelf in the   corner for items nearing their   expiration date, which made it much   easier for me to find them. So, in 2023  , when I was 35, I decided to do this   again. This time, it was a success. As   marked in yellow on this receipt,   that's the date I purchased it, and the   date on this packaging is the   expiration date. These two dates being   the same proves that I bought it on   that very day. It went very smoothly;   on January 1st, I bought a box of   cookies expiring on January 1st, so I   ate those cookies. On January 2nd, I   bought a bag of flour expiring on   January 2nd, and I ate that; on January   3rd, I bought a carton of milk expiring   on January 3rd, so I drank that milk.   And so on—that's how the whole year   went. I would usually go to the five   supermarkets nearest to my place and   two discount stores. If I couldn't find   anything, I'd go to two large   supermarkets in Central and Admiralty.   If I still couldn't find anything, I'd   go to Causeway Bay, checking smaller   markets along the way as well. If I   still didn't find anything, I'd cross   the harbor to Kowloon or take the MTR   to the Southern District. This item was   found at a discount store downstairs;   this one is the largest supermarket   near my place, and I usually go there   first thing in the morning when it   opens. At Marks&Spencer, I found a   carton of eggs here. I found a bottle   of beer, so I drank that bottle of beer  . Some supermarkets didn't provide   receipts, but they had the time on   their screens, so I took a picture of   the screen. Hong Kong is one of the   most globalized places; it has goods   from all over the world, including some   very niche items you don't usually see,   and I want to show them to you. For   example: Turkish tea, Egyptian juice,   Czech beer, German pickles, Jordanian   olives, Mexican bean dip—this one is   the cheapest of the whole series, only   one dollar—Bulgarian peppers, Indian   baking soda, Ukrainian peas, and   Nigerian sesame seeds. Over this year,   I found 286 items, and there were 79   days......79 days where I didn't find   anything. Here, I feel that not finding   anything is also part of the work,   because the" nothingness "of not   finding is different from the"   nothingness "of not searching or   putting in effort. I put in the effort   but got no results; I feel it has a   strong sense of presence. Looking back,   in the first month, the hardest times   were when I couldn't find anything for   three days straight. In the second   month, there were also times I couldn't   find anything for three days. The rest   were luckier, only one or two days   without finding anything. If I couldn't   find anything, I had nothing to eat. So  , as this project was nearing its end,   I had a check-up, and sure enough,   things weren't normal. Red blood cell   count was too low, white blood cell   count was too low, anemia, all sorts of   issues, but the biggest problem was my   heart. The EKG doctor, as soon as they   looked at the results, had a very   serious expression and said," Your   T-wave is abnormal. "I didn't even know   what a T-wave was. He told me to go see   a cardiologist for an examination. So I   went to see the cardiologist. He asked   if I was anxious, and I immediately   understood. Of course I was anxious; if   I couldn't find anything, I had nothing   to eat. If I didn't find anything for   one day, I felt anxious; two days, I   couldn't sleep; three days, I felt like   I was going crazy. Last year, I held an   exhibition; this is what the exhibition   looked like. I kept all these items.   The food was eaten, but the packaging   boxes remained. Each item had spent a   day with me. That day was very pure; it   was just me and it. I only looked for   it, only bought it, only ate it. I find   this kind of bond to be very beautiful.   Moreover, that day was the last day of   its existence. So, in this work, that   time, that date, is not just a measure   of time, but a unique, singular   existence. So, what is time? I believe   time is the present moment. For example  , right now, I am here with all of you.   Tomorrow we will be separated. No need   for tomorrow; after I finish speaking   in a moment, we will be separated. This   immediacy, this uniqueness, this   irreversibility—that is what makes   time beautiful, not how long you live   or how long your life is. That is not   beauty. The four stories I shared with   you above have something in common.   They are all related to" loss. "Mother   and child. My mother passed away. The   letter from Hong Kong. I went to Hong   Kong and couldn't adapt. Heavy is the   night. I felt a shared fate with those   sleeping in McDonald's—homeless,   connected by fate, reflecting on the   passage of time and the sighs of life.   Usually, when we encounter a dilemma,   there are two ways to handle it. One is   avoidance: the dilemma is there, I am   here, and I ignore it. Like parallel   lines. The other is to resist: the   difficulty is there, and I must defeat   it, change it, or overcome it, like a   vertical line. Is there a third way? I   think there is, which is transformation  . When a dilemma arrives. It was   originally like this, but if I shift my   own angle or perspective, this   difficulty then takes on a different   form. Usually, we use painting, music,   poetry, or film to transform it, but   these methods all have barriers. Is   there a way without barriers? I think   there is. And that is, living. The"   living "I mean here is different from   what we usually say, like eating when   hungry, sleeping when tired, or making   money when broke. The living I refer to   here requires subjectivity; it must be   conscious, subjective, and a life   transformed and shaped by standards of   beauty. To distinguish between these   two kinds of living, I have given my   way of living a name:" Living. "If you   know a little about art history,   you’ll be familiar with the term"   performance art. "So, what is   performance art? It means we turn our   body into language, the body becomes   the primary scene; from then on,   wherever the body is, art is there. So,   can we add narration to the foundation   of performance art, treating life as   the primary scene? From then on,   wherever life is, art is there. This is   what I’m talking about today:" Living  . "Tha nks.
