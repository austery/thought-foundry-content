Here are twelve types of bonds, from
government loans to hurricane bets,
and what each one means for your money.
Number one: Treasuries. Buy a Treasury,
and you're lending money to the
U.S. government. Think of a bond as an
I.O.U.
The borrower gets your money now and
promises payments in return.
You're a lender, not an owner.
You don't get a little piece of the White
House.
Suppose you lend a thousand dollars.
The borrower promises extra money for
using your loan.
That extra money is interest.
In this deal, you receive regular
interest payments, then your thousand
dollars back on an agreed date.
Those regular payments are called
coupons.
The amount due at the end is the face
value.
And the repayment date is called
maturity.
Three names, but one simple question:
what do I get paid, and when?
Treasuries come in different lengths.
Bills last a year or less.
Notes last two to ten years.
Bonds last twenty or thirty.
Bills usually give you your gain at the
end.
Notes and bonds pay interest every six
months.
The U.S. government is generally
considered very likely to repay.
But what if you need your money before
the repayment date?
You can sell the bond to another person.
They pay you now, and the future payments
go to them.
Here's the catch. Suppose your bond pays
twenty dollars a year.
A new bond, just as likely to repay and
due on the same date, now pays
fifty dollars a year for the same price.
Which would a buyer prefer?
Yours still promises the same payments,
but you may have to sell it for
less to make it attractive.
So there are two different questions.
Will the borrower pay me?
And what could I sell the bond for today?
A dependable borrower doesn't guarantee a
steady sale price.
There's one more problem. You get your
thousand dollars back, but shopping
has become more expensive. The money buys
less.
That's where our next bond comes in.
Number two: TIPS. These are U.S.
government bonds designed to help protect
your money's buying power.
Imagine taking the same money to the
supermarket and coming home with less
food. When prices generally rise, that's
inflation.
Ordinary bond payments can stay the same
while the things you want to buy
get dearer. With TIPS, the amount the
government owes you changes with an
official measure of consumer prices.
If that measure rises five percent, a
thousand dollars becomes a thousand
and fifty. The interest percentage stays
the same, but it's now applied to
a bigger amount. So the dollar payment
grows too.
The adjustment can also go down if prices
fall.
But on the final repayment date, the
government pays at least the original
thousand dollars. That protection covers
the original amount promised, not
extra money you might have paid another
investor to buy the bond.
And remember our two questions.
Protection at the repayment date doesn't
guarantee today's sale price.
Sell early, and you could still receive
less than you paid.
TIPS change the amount owed to help with
rising prices.
Next, let's change who's borrowing: from
the national government to the
town down the road.
Number three: municipal bonds. These are
loans to state and local
borrowers, such as a city.
A city needs new water pipes.
Investors lend it money, the pipes get
built, and the city pays the loan
back over time. The same idea can help
fund schools and roads.
But where does the repayment money come
from?
Some bonds rely on taxes the local
government collects.
These are called general obligation
bonds.
Others rely on money from a particular
project, such as the water bills
households pay. Those are revenue bonds.
Same broad family, different source of
money.
Many municipal bonds have another
attraction: the interest isn't subject to
U.S. federal income tax. State and local
tax rules can differ, and there
are exceptions. Why care? A bond paying
more interest could
leave you with less after tax.
A bond paying less could let you keep
more.
It's the money left in your pocket that
matters.
But a tax advantage doesn't repair an
empty budget.
The borrower can still fail to make the
promised payments.
That's called default. The next borrowers
are companies, and some are much
more dependable than others.
Number four: investment-grade corporate
bonds.
Corporate simply means the borrower is a
company.
Imagine a company wants a new factory.
It can borrow from bond investors and
promise to pay them back with
interest. If the factory does
brilliantly, the lenders still aren't
part-owners. They get the payments their
bonds promise.
So how do you judge whether the company
can repay?
Specialist companies study borrowers and
give them credit ratings.
Think of a rating as a repayment report
card.
Investment grade is the stronger-rated
group.
A good report card is useful.
It isn't a guarantee. The rating can be
wrong, the business can get worse,
and the company can still miss payments.
A famous name on the building doesn't
change that.
If you trust a company more to repay, you
may accept less interest from it.
A less dependable borrower usually has to
offer more to persuade you to
lend. This label tells you about the
borrower's rating.
It doesn't tell you whether your bond's
sale price will rise or fall.
Now let's meet the companies on the
riskier side of that report card.
Number five: high-yield bonds, also known
as junk bonds.
Unfortunate branding. You're still buying
a real promise to repay a loan.
These bonds are rated below investment
grade.
Imagine two companies asking you for the
same loan.
One comfortably pays its bills.
The other is already struggling.
You would probably want a better deal
before lending to the second one.
That's the bargain. A higher yield means
a better return on the price you
pay, if the promised payments arrive.
The bigger offer is there to persuade you
to accept more risk.
But a promise isn't money in your pocket.
A borrower can miss interest payments or
fail to return all your loan.
That loss can wipe out years of extra
income.
Many high-yield bonds do repay in full;
the danger is that repayment is
less dependable. So far, we've asked
who's borrowing and how likely they
are to pay. Next, we're changing the
payment rules.
These labels can overlap: a company bond
can have a credit rating and
several of the features we're about to
meet.
Number six: zero-coupon bonds. With
these, you wait for the money at the
end. Imagine paying six hundred dollars
today for a bond that promises a
thousand dollars in fifteen years.
No interest cheques arrive along the way.
Your gain is the four-hundred-dollar gap,
over the whole fifteen years, if
the borrower pays as promised.
That's why it's called zero coupon.
The regular interest payments we called
coupons are missing.
You're buying a future payment for less
money today.
This can fit a future expense with a
known date.
The catch is the wait.
Remember the old bond competing with a
better new offer?
If new deals become more attractive, a
buyer may offer less for your old
bond. With a zero-coupon bond, all the
promised money is still waiting at
the far end. You cannot take an earlier
payment and put it into a better
new deal, because there are no earlier
payments.
That makes its sale price especially
sensitive to changing interest rates
compared with an otherwise similar bond
that pays some money earlier.
And, depending on the bond and your tax
rules, you can owe tax on interest
before the cash reaches you.
No cheque doesn't always mean no tax
bill.
What if the payment could change as
interest rates change?
That's our next bond.
Number seven: floating-rate bonds. Here,
the interest payment can move up
or down. A fixed-rate bond keeps the same
interest percentage.
A floating-rate contract names an
interest rate to follow.
On set dates, the deal checks that rate
and adjusts what you receive.
Think of the payments as water from a
tap.
A fixed payment gives the same flow.
With the floating payment, the contract's
rule turns the tap up or down.
If the rate it follows rises, your
payment can rise.
If that rate falls, your payment can fall
too.
This helps explain why floating-rate
bonds usually react less sharply to
changing rates than similar fixed-rate
bonds.
The old bond can start paying more as new
offers improve.
It isn't stuck with exactly the same
payment.
But the tap has rules.
Payments might only change on certain
dates, and the contract may limit the
change. The borrower can still fail to
pay.
Floating doesn't mean unsinkable. You're
giving up certainty about future
interest payments in return for payments
that can adjust.
Next comes a different choice: stop being
a lender and become an owner.
Number eight: convertible bonds. A
typical convertible lets you swap
the bond for shares in the company.
A share is a small piece of ownership.
So you begin as a lender, but the deal
gives you a possible route into
becoming a part-owner instead. Imagine a
bond that can be exchanged for
twenty shares. If the company grows and
those shares become attractive
enough, you may choose the shares over
the bond's promised payments.
You hand over the bond and receive the
shares instead.
You don't keep both as a free bonus.
If the shares don't look attractive, you
may keep the bond, depending on
the contract. But you still need the
company to pay.
The option to become an owner doesn't
rescue your money if the business
fails. That choice has value.
Investors may accept less interest
because they also get a chance to switch
into shares. The contract decides who can
make the switch and when; some
deals have different rules. For the
typical convertible, that useful choice
belongs to the investor. In the next
bond, the borrower gets an important
choice instead.
Number nine: callable bonds. Here, the
borrower can choose to pay you back
early, if the contract allows it.
Suppose a company borrowed when interest
rates were high.
Later, it can get a cheaper loan.
So it repays the expensive old bond and
borrows again at the lower rate.
That's refinancing: replacing an old loan
with a new one.
Lovely for the company. Less lovely if
you were enjoying those interest
payments. You get your loan money back,
but the good payments stop.
To replace that income, you may have to
accept less interest or take more
risk. That's called reinvestment risk.
Getting repaid can be inconvenient when
the next deal isn't as good.
Investors may ask for more interest at
the start to accept that
possibility. The borrower can't simply
ignore the agreement.
The contract says when early repayment is
allowed and how much must be
paid. Disney once sold bonds with a
hundred-year repayment date, but the
contract allowed repayment earlier. A
hundred-year deadline didn't promise
a hundred years of interest cheques.
That bond had a distant deadline.
The next one has no set repayment date at
all.
Number ten: perpetual bonds. The borrower
has no set date when
it must return the original loan.
Imagine a loan with the repayment-date
box left empty.
Interest payments can continue under the
deal's rules, but there isn't a
normal finish date when your original
money must come back.
That doesn't mean you've found an
immortal money machine.
The borrower can fail. Some contracts
allow interest payments to be
postponed or skipped. Others let the
borrower repay the loan early.
If you want your money before the
borrower pays back, you may need to sell
the bond to someone else.
And that buyer decides what they're
willing to pay.
Britain used government debt with no set
repayment date for centuries.
In twenty fifteen, it announced repayment
of its last bonds of that kind.
No required end date didn't mean
repayment was forbidden.
So perpetual tells you about the missing
deadline.
It doesn't guarantee endless income.
Next, instead of asking when the money
arrives, let's ask where it comes
from.
Number eleven: asset-backed bonds. Start
with a mortgage: a
loan used to buy a home.
Homeowners make payments on their
mortgages.
Imagine the payments from lots of those
loans flowing into a shared pot.
Investors can buy the right to receive
some of that money.
With mortgages, this is called a
mortgage-backed security.
The pot explains the basic idea.
Many borrowers make payments, and
investors receive money from those
payments. Other arrangements can use car
loans or money people owe on
credit cards. But who gets served first?
The deal may pay one group of investors
first, then pay another from what's
left. If too little money comes in, the
second group can miss out sooner.
Putting loans together doesn't make the
borrowers better at paying.
There's also the early-repayment problem.
Homeowners may replace their old
mortgages with cheaper loans.
Money then returns to the investors
sooner, and the interest payments they
expected can stop early. The money
doesn't even have to come from loans.
Music earns payments when it's sold or
used.
Those payments are called royalties.
In nineteen ninety-seven, future
royalties from David Bowie's music helped
support a bond deal, raising money
upfront.
Different source, same idea: future
payments help raise money today.
Our final bond uses a very different
bargain.
Your money may be needed when disaster
strikes.
Number twelve: catastrophe bonds. An
insurer or government wants help
paying for a major disaster.
Think of an emergency pot funded by
investors.
They get paid for agreeing to put their
money at risk.
If the disaster described in the deal
happens, some or all of that money
can go to the insurer or government
instead of back to the investors.
The exact condition matters. Imagine a
contract covering a hurricane of a
specified strength in a particular area.
A storm outside that area doesn't meet
that test.
A weaker storm inside it doesn't either.
Other contracts use different tests, so a
hurricane on television doesn't
tell you what happens to every
catastrophe bond.
If no qualifying event occurs during the
deal, the original money is
normally returned at the end.
If the condition is met, the investor can
lose part of it, or all of it.
That's why the investor gets paid:
they're accepting a particular disaster
risk. This is an emergency pot their
money might never come back from.
We've gone from government borrowing to
hurricane risk.
But one danger kept appearing throughout:
needing your money back before
the bond pays. A real bank learned how
serious that can be.
Remember the difference between getting
repaid and selling early?
Silicon Valley Bank shows why it matters.
Customers put money into their bank
accounts.
The bank invested heavily in bonds and
other investments that would pay
over a longer period. But customers could
ask for their money back much
sooner. Then interest rates rose.
New bonds offered better payments, so the
bank's older investments became
less attractive to buyers. Their sale
prices fell.
The promised future payments and the
price somebody would pay today were
different things. That becomes a problem
when you need cash now.
Selling early can mean accepting a loss.
In March twenty twenty-three, the bank's
parent company announced a large
investment sale and a loss.
A rapid bank run followed: customers
rushed to withdraw their money.
Silicon Valley Bank failed on March
tenth.
The official review found failures by
managers and supervisors, including
poor preparation for changing interest
rates and sudden withdrawals.
This wasn't simply a case of every bond
borrower refusing to repay.
The bank had put money into longer-term
investments while depending on
customers leaving their money in their
accounts.
The lesson comes back to our two
questions.
Can the borrower pay as promised?
And what happens if I need my money
sooner?
Those are different risks, even when the
bond looks dependable.
So that's twelve types of bonds.
You don't need twelve separate
dictionaries.
Follow the money. First, who's borrowing?
It could be a government, a city or a
company.
Then ask how dependable the promise is.
A credit rating helps judge that, but
doesn't guarantee it.
Next, when do you get paid, and what can
change?
You might wait until the end, receive
payments that rise or fall, swap into
shares, or get repaid earlier than you
wanted.
Some deals have no set final repayment
date.
Finally, what puts your money at risk?
Prices rising, a borrower failing, a
forced early sale, or even a
disaster named in the contract.
Several labels can describe the same
bond.
A company can issue an investment-grade
bond with floating payments and an
early-repayment option. Whether it's a
government loan, music income or a
hurricane bet, the useful question is the
same: who has my money, what have
they promised, and what could change the
deal?
