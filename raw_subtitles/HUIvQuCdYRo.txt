Here are 10 tricks Walmart uses to make you
spend more at the store that
promises the lowest prices.
Number one, the rollback sign.
It tells you the price has come down.
It doesn't tell you that nobody else sells
the item for less.
Walmart describes rollbacks as temporary
reductions.
The old price gives you a comparison
immediately.
You see a saving against that number, and
checking a competitor starts to
feel unnecessary. The leap happens when
lower than before becomes lowest
available. Those are two different claims
conveniently wearing the same red
sign
Number two, digital price tags.
These replace paper labels with electronic
displays, making approved prices
quicker to update. Walmart says the shelf
price is the same for everyone
standing in front of it.
It also says digital labels display
centrally approved prices rather than
deciding them. So how can the same item
have two prices?
Walmart says prices can differ between
stores because of local costs,
competition, and other market conditions.
The practical issue is that a price is
attached to this item in this store at
this time. Remembering a cheaper price
somewhere else doesn't make this one
match it. The label may be electronic, but
the useful question remains
wonderfully old-fashioned. What am I
actually being charged?
Number three, Great Value beside the brand
name.
The shelf gives you an easy demonstration
of Walmart's cheap price image.
Here's the familiar package, and here's
Walmart's own brand for less.
That can be a sensible substitution.
A product doesn't become better simply
because its advertising budget is
bigger. But now you've made a saving inside
Walmart, so Walmart has won the
comparison before another store even enters
it.
Suppose you switch to the cheaper cereal.
You may then feel more comfortable adding
something else because you've been
careful with the groceries. The saving on
one item becomes permission to
relax on the next. Imagine a twelve-ounce
box for three dollars beside an
eighteen-ounce box for four dollars.
The three-dollar box wins the big number
comparison, but each ounce costs
twenty-five cents against about twenty-two
cents in the larger box.
Putting both prices on the same scale
changes which one looks cheaper.
The package price was answering a different
question.
The relevant details still matter: package
size, ingredients, and whether you
actually like it. A cheaper box that nobody
eats isn't especially economical.
Great Value doesn't have to beat every
alternative everywhere.
It can reinforce the feeling that you're
already shopping intelligently,
right where Walmart wants you to keep
shopping
Number four, the pharmacy trip.
Walmart advertises selected 30-day generic
prescriptions starting at $4,
with conditions and some state price
differences.
A low price on something you regularly need
gives you a reason to return.
You're already there for a prescription.
Picking up milk saves another stop.
Then you remember laundry detergent, and
perhaps the batteries you've been
meaning to buy. One errand becomes several
purchases.
That convenience can be valuable.
It also means Walmart doesn't have to
persuade you to make a fresh shopping
trip every time. The pharmacy supplies the
reason to arrive, and the rest of
the store is available once you do.
Say a pharmacy trip saves you $6 compared
with your usual option.
On the way out, you add a $12 item you
hadn't planned to buy.
You've kept the $6 saving, but your
spending has still risen by $6 overall.
The store benefits from what joins the
prescription, even when the
prescription itself is a good deal.
A genuine saving can bring you through the
door without limiting what goes
through the checkout.
Number five, checkout candy. You didn't
drive to Walmart for one small
chocolate bar. That's precisely why its
location matters.
At checkout, you're close enough to pick it
up, you've already committed to
paying, and you're waiting with little else
to do.
The product gets another chance after the
shopping list has apparently
finished. A small treat also looks
insignificant beside a cart full of
groceries. You carefully compared
detergent, so adding one snack may feel
like a reward for having done the sensible
work.
The tempting object is right there at a
moment when saying yes takes almost
no effort. The shelf in the candy aisle
waits for people who go looking for
candy. The checkout display gets to ask
everybody else.
Your shopping trip has an ending.
The store would prefer to squeeze one more
sentence into it
Number six, self-checkout. You scan the
item, put it in the bag, and move to
the next one. Your attention follows the
task one product at a time.
The checkout is also a screen the retailer
controls, and Walmart Connect has
offered advertising on those screens.
A place where you finish buying becomes
another place where brands can reach
you. An ad doesn't have to send you running
back through the aisles
immediately. It can put a product in your
head for the next visit.
The payment moment is still part of the
sales environment.
Meanwhile, scanning correctly and checking
the total are different jobs.
The machine can register every item while
you barely consider what the basket
has become. The useful distinction is
between completing the checkout and
reviewing the purchase. One happens because
the screen asks you to do it.
The other requires your attention
Number seven, Walmart Plus. The standard
membership is $98 a year
or $12.95 a month before applicable tax.
It offers benefits including delivery and
shipping with different conditions
for each. Once you've paid, using Walmart
again can feel like getting your
money's worth. Going elsewhere means
leaving your benefits unused, even if
another store has a better price on this
particular order.
The membership changes which costs feel
important.
A delivery charge you avoid is easy to
notice.
A small difference spread across several
grocery items is harder to hold in
your head. Imagine avoiding a $7 delivery
charge, then paying 50 cents
more on each of 20 grocery items.
Those small differences add up to $10.
The visible delivery saving has been
overtaken inside the basket.
To judge that order, you need the delivered
total from each store with the
same products and quantities rather than
the delivery line alone.
There can be real savings if the benefits
fit how you shop, but the fee
you've already paid doesn't make the next
basket automatically cheapest.
Walmart is selling convenience and a reason
to become your default.
A membership works particularly well for
the business when deciding where to
shop stops being a decision you make each
time.
Number eight, the store as an
advertisement.
Walmart Connect sells advertising that
reaches shoppers through screens,
audio, and other experiences around the
store.
A product can therefore become familiar
while you're shopping for something
else. You hear about it, see it again, and
then encounter it near the point
where you could buy it.
None of those appearances tells you it's
the cheapest choice in its category.
Prominence and value are separate things.
Think about two cereal brands you've never
tried.
One has appeared on a screen and in an
announcement during this visit.
The other is just a box on a shelf.
When you finally compare them, one already
feels familiar.
That earlier exposure can enter the
decision before you've compared the
ingredients, package size, or price per
ounce.
The store can help you find products while
also selling brands access to your
attention. You're comparing groceries
inside a place where somebody may have
paid to shape what you notice first.
Number nine, weighed item pricing.
Here, the number on the package isn't
always a simple price for one identical
unit. You may be paying by the pound, which
makes the weight part of the
calculation. A class action alleged that
certain Walmart meat, seafood, and
bagged citrus purchases cost shoppers more
than the lowest advertised price.
Walmart denied wrongdoing and agreed to a
forty-five million dollar
settlement. The broader problem is easy to
see.
A large discount sticker attracts
attention, while the unit price, package
weight, and checkout total require a little
arithmetic.
For example, two pounds at four dollars a
pound should cost eight dollars.
A cheaper-looking price per pound doesn't
help if the recorded weight is
wrong. The label has several numbers doing
different jobs.
Looking only at the most attractive one can
leave the number you actually pay
unchecked.
Number 10, everyday low price.
This is the promise holding the other nine
together.
Walmart wants low prices to feel like a
property of the store, so you don't
have to investigate every item.
That trust saves time. Comparing a whole
cart across several stores is work,
and driving to each one costs time and
fuel.
It can be perfectly reasonable to choose a
store that usually works for you.
The risk is turning usually good value into
always the cheapest.
Walmart's price match policy has limits.
It doesn't offer a general promise to beat
every competitor's price.
A rollback, a cheap own brand product, and
an inexpensive prescription can
all be genuine. They still can't prove the
price of the headphones you added
on the way out. The strongest trick isn't
necessarily hiding a high price.
It's establishing a low price reputation so
thoroughly that you stop asking
about the next one
Costco builds that trust in a very
different setting with cheap food,
membership rewards, and a warehouse full of
surprises.
