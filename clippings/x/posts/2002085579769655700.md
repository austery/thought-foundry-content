## Original text

<pre>The regime changed quietly: efficiency stopped being a virtue. It became a liability.

Taipei. An Ivy campus. Australia. Different contexts, same structure.

High-order systems are now colliding with unmodelable, individual tail shocks.

Not because the world “lost control.” Because we optimized control into fragility.

For three decades, we engineered the mean.

We centralized supply chains, standardized processes, compressed redundancy, and celebrated utilization.
It worked, until the tail started pricing itself into daily life.

Deglobalization is not a mood. It is risk budgeting. Political consolidation is not stability. It is correlation. Identity and belief hardening into tribes is not culture. It is narrative volatility.

Semiconductors are the cleanest mirror.

The world concentrated critical compute, advanced packaging, and geopolitical exposure into too few places.

In the mean, it looks like dominance.
In the tail, it is a single-point failure with a global blast radius.

This is where Intel matters, even if you dislike the stock. Not as “the next TSMC,” but as a second pole.

A partial US-based stack changes the distribution: more routes, more optionality, less world-wide correlation to one island and one strait.

In a tail-driven regime, partial redundancy is not a nice-to-have. It is a valuation input.

The market is slowly repricing from growth premium to resilience premium.
Not because resilience is inspiring. Because it is scarce, measurable, and survivable.

The question is no longer whether a shock happens. The question is whether you still have options when it does.

Company resilience is redundancy, multi-path supply, and onshore capability.

Portfolio resilience is liquidity, hedges, position sizing, and detoxing from single-node dependence.

Mean returns are easy to model.
Tails decide careers.</pre>
