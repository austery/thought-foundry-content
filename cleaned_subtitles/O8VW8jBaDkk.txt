On Monday , October 19 , 1987 , in the Chicago S & P futures trading pit , an asset management firm held a massive stack of sell orders . These orders weren't made up on the fly ; they were calculated by computer models , dictating exactly how much to sell as the market dropped , without missing a single contract . A company trader was responsible for delivering these orders to the pit . His job was usually simple : when the model said sell , he sold . You could think of him as a servant to the computer model . But that day , he stopped . Even though the model kept calling for more sells , he simply refused to send them out . He later explained , " In my experience , if all these orders were sold , I’m certain the market would drop to zero . " Just think about it : what did this company rely on to make a living ? It was that very model . The model had only one rule : if it drops , sell , no hesitation . Now , the person supposed to execute the rules was the first one to lose his nerve . This machine , designed to automatically dump stocks according to the rules , was held back at the most critical moment by the very person who understood it best . But his attempt to stop it couldn't save the market . By the close of that day , the Dow Jones had plunged 508 points , a drop of 22.6 % . The company was called LOR , formed by the initials of its three founders . The " L " at the beginning was a university professor ; he wasn't a trader by trade , nor a fund manager — he was just a teacher . He was the one who invented this machine , and his name was Leland . In a previous episode , we talked about October 28th and 29th , 1929 , when the Dow Jones index fell a total of 23 % over two days — the worst period in US stock market history at the time . Yet , on October 19 , 1987 , the market dropped nearly the same amount in just a single day . This single day's decline was nearly double that of the worst day in 1929 . That day , the New York Stock Exchange traded over 600 million shares worth $ 21 billion , nearly doubling the previous record . Nearly 2,000 stocks fell , while only 52 rose , most of which were gold and silver mining companies . In times of chaos , everyone just wanted to hold onto their gold and silver . Counting from the close of the previous Tuesday , in less than a week , the Dow had lost nearly a third of its value , with roughly $ 1 trillion in market wealth evaporating . So the question is , who did this ? You might guess that it was speculators borrowing money to trade stocks , gamblers just repeating the events of 1929 . But this time , the ones who gave that final push over the cliff were those most afraid of risk : pension funds and corporate retirement accounts . They had bought an insurance policy for their stocks . On Monday alone , this insurance forced them to sell 33,000 S & P futures contracts , about 4 billion dollars , while dumping billions more in stocks directly onto the New York market . That August , a New York University professor wrote a line that I think is the most accurate assessment of this crash : this disaster wasn't caused by too much speculation , but by too much hedging . When one person buys insurance , it's cautious ; when everyone buys the same insurance , it turns into a disaster . This insurance was conceived 11 years earlier by a professor who couldn't sleep at night , taking him only about two hours to develop . In September 1976 , at Berkeley , a 35 - year-old man lay in bed , tossing and turning ; what kept him awake was money . His resume was impressive — he was the classic high achiever , with a master's from the LSE and a Ph.D. in economics from Harvard — but the U.S. economy was a mess back then . From 1973 to 1975 , the economy was in recession , with unemployment hitting 9 % due to the oil crisis , which had quadrupled prices . He was a junior faculty member , an assistant professor , worried about his job security , and the pay for teaching wasn't exactly great . He later described that night quite bluntly : life was getting tough , and it was time to come up with an invention . An invention for what ? A seed had long been planted in his mind , a seed sown by his older brother . His brother was a high-level executive at an asset management firm . In 1974 , the U.S. stock market hit bottom , with the Dow Jones dropping nearly 40 % in two years — the worst stretch since the Great Depression . Many institutions pulled their money out of the market , determined never to return . At a family gathering , his brother complained , " If only we could buy insurance for stocks , then no one would have to sell at the bottom and we could catch the subsequent rebound . " The young professor took those words to heart . But the problem was too difficult , and he had other projects , so it remained shelved until that night in September 1976 , when he got out of bed and sat down at his desk . He later said that it took him about two hours to figure it out . What did he figure out ? What my brother wanted actually had a name long ago : it’s called a put option . The logic is actually the same as buying car insurance : you pay a premium , and if you crash your car , the insurance company pays . A put option is also essentially trading a premium , which is the premium you pay to purchase this option . In exchange for a guaranteed selling price , if the stock price drops , you can still sell it at that price . But where is the catch ? In 1976 , you couldn't buy these in America . The Chicago Board Options Exchange had only opened three years earlier , and they only traded call options . Everyone thought put options were too dangerous and even a bit unpatriotic , so they didn't sell them . But that night , he remembered a 1973 paper by two professors . The paper hinted at an idea : even if no one in the market is selling an option , you can piece one together yourself . By holding stocks and cash , you sell a small portion when the price drops and buy it back when it rises . As long as you trade accurately enough , the final result is similar to having bought that insurance policy . Since there was no such policy on the market , he decided to create one himself . This professor who couldn't sleep is the Leland I mentioned at the beginning . The next morning , Leland realized a problem : he understood the logic , but he couldn't calculate the math himself . He went to a colleague at Berkeley . Upon hearing this , the colleague's first reaction was , " Oh , why didn't I think of that ? " That colleague's name was Rubinstein . The two sat down and sketched out a plan : you take a basket of stocks , watch the price , sell a small piece according to the formula when it drops , and buy it back when it rises , repeating this until the policy expires . How much to sell and at what price ? It's all calculated by the formula , requiring no human brainpower . They called this strategy " dynamic hedging , " while everyone else called it " portfolio insurance . " Friends who trade A-shares might be familiar with " grid trading " : buy a little when it drops , and sell a little when it rises . Portfolio insurance is the exact opposite : sell a little when it drops , and buy a little when it rises . To put it plainly , it's chasing gains and cutting losses . Except , this is done by using the most sophisticated mathematical formulas to chase gains and cut losses meticulously . This policy also has a prerequisite . In that paper , the two professors wrote down a few ideal conditions . The first is that stock prices move smoothly without jumping . Another is that there are no transaction fees for buying and selling . Anyone who has spent a day on the trading floor could tell that these two points simply don't align with reality . The two of them were well aware of this , and later , when speaking to clients , they made the risks clear upfront : if a major crisis occurred and the market plunged , this insurance policy wouldn't work . Leland even gave an example . The Soviets invading Iran to cut off Middle Eastern oil — keep these words in mind . Eleven years later , it actually happened , just in a different way , and now it has played out again . But there was another assumption they truly believed in . They were convinced that they could keep selling off their shares . They wouldn't crash the price because the market is full of smart people who would immediately recognize it as a mechanical , programmatic sell-off . It wouldn't signal any bad news . Seeing it as a bargain , smart people would naturally step in to catch the falling knife . His logic was : " I dare to jump because I believe a crowd of people is standing below , waiting to catch me . " But what if that crowd below had also bought the exact same insurance policy ? Then who is left to catch them ? It's just like the talk about hedge funds today ; many claim they provide massive liquidity , but you know as well as I do that when liquidity is truly needed , these hedge funds won't be there to buy . At the time , this wasn't just their idea ; the entire field of finance thought this way . How so ? For the formulas to work , one must assume that humans are always rational . In 1979 , they turned this into a set of rules , dictating when to sell and how much to sell . As long as the assumption held , this basket of stocks would have a floor that it could never break through . Actually , according to the author of the book " Devil Take the Hindmost , " every modern market crash hides a seemingly sophisticated financial device that few truly understand : in 1907 it was trust companies , in 1929 it was the nesting-doll funds we discussed last time , and in 1987 , it was this very insurance policy . The product was built , but now it had to be sold . Leland carried a recommendation letter from a famous Silicon Valley asset manager and traveled to the Eastern and Midwestern U.S. , specifically targeting bank trust departments and other money-managing institutions . Every meeting lasted a long time , often running over schedule by several hours . He later recalled it with a sense of pride , but when he walked out of the conference rooms , he hadn't signed a single contract . He went back and waited by the phone , but later said , " Good heavens , not a single person called . " It seems this assistant professor was not much of a salesman ; perhaps the longer the meeting , the more it means people didn't understand it at all ? Think about it : after listening for several hours without paying a dime , did they even understand it ? In the fall of 1980 , someone attended a financial seminar at Berkeley . He realized immediately that this could make a fortune and quit his job on the spot . In 1981 , he became the head of the firm , and the company was renamed , combining the letters of the three founders — the " LOR " we mentioned at the start . He could speak in plain language , breaking down intimidating mathematics into bite-sized pieces clients could actually understand . He won over almost every client he approached . Soon , the company was managing $ 135 million . By 1982 , major corporations like Honeywell came knocking , and the assets under protection exceeded $ 250 million . In 1982 , the Chicago Mercantile Exchange launched the S & P 500 stock index futures . L realized immediately that he didn't have to keep selling clients ' stocks one by one ; he could just sell futures . Futures were like slices of the entire market — you could sell as much as you wanted . In fact , as early as the beginning of 1983 , some people had already spotted the flaws . A researcher from Prudential's pension department attended an LOR presentation and wrote down several concerns . The most critical one was that if too many people used this strategy , the price would snowball — if prices fell , everyone would ... but at the time , nobody took it seriously . By October 1987 , the assets protected by portfolio insurance had grown about 90 - fold . LOR itself protected $ 5 billion , licensed its method to protect another $ 45 billion , and competitors with identical products protected $ 40 billion , totaling a full $ 90 billion . At the start , they couldn't even sign one client a year , but reaching $ 90 billion meant the product had suddenly become a viral hit . Mathematics alone wasn't enough ; you also needed something else — a massive bull market that made people dare to put their money back into stocks . It was a group of " barbarians " that ignited this bull market . On August 12 , 1982 , the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 776.92 . It had fallen for eight consecutive days , dropping lower than its 1964 close — eighteen years of gains wiped out . Nobody knew it then , but that was the bottom . From that day on , the Dow never closed that low again . Two days later , the Federal Reserve cut interest rates , and the bull market began — climbing 34.7 % in 141 days . The timing of LOR's founding was perfect ; just before the market bottomed out , two men closed a deal : one was a former Treasury Secretary , and the other was a tax accountant . They set their sights on a greeting card company , best known for a lazy , fat cat named Garfield . In January 1982 , the pair spent 80 million dollars to buy the company . They each put up 330,000 , totaling just one million , while the rest was borrowed . It’s like putting down one dollar of your own to borrow 79 more . A year and a half later , in May 1983 , the company went public with a valuation of 290 million . Both of them walked away with 70 million dollars each . Just think , in such a short time , 330,000 turned into 70 million — a return of over 200 times in just 17 months ! Wall Street caught on instantly . Cheap companies were everywhere , and banks were lining up to lend . The era of the leveraged buyout began right then . The Garfield deal was a voluntary sale , but for those who didn't want to sell , they just played hardball . There was an oil tycoon in Oklahoma who started as a geologist before going out on his own . He realized early on that drilling for oil was too slow ; it was much faster to just buy an oil company . In 1982 , he targeted an oil company more than twice his size — quite an appetite , right ? He offered 50 dollars a share . He had done the math : at that price , the oil reserves he was buying cost just over 5 dollars a barrel . At that time , finding a new barrel of oil cost 15 dollars , while the market price was over 30. Wall Street had become the cheapest oil field in America . In the end , another oil giant swooped in and bought the company out from under him . He didn't succeed in buying it , but his firm flipped the shares and netted a profit of 45 million dollars . This oil tycoon was T. Boone Pickens , whom we also mentioned in our group portrait of corporate raiders . The following year , he set his sights on Gulf Oil , the fifth-largest oil company in the world . In March 1984 , Chevron stepped in and bought all of Gulf . Pickens failed again , but he and his firm still made 760 million dollars , while helping other Gulf shareholders pocket an extra 5.5 billion . Whether they actually bought the company no longer mattered ; once the " barbarians " knocked on the door , the stock price would start to soar . The most typical example of this in China was the 2015 Baoneng takeover attempt of Vanke ; at the time , the media branded Baoneng as " barbarians . " Among these barbarians , there is another figure who is a completely different breed from Pickens . This person was born in Queens , New York , from a family of opera singers . He was quite tall , studied philosophy at Princeton , and later attended medical school but didn't finish . His game was different : he would set his sights on a company , quietly buy up a large chunk of shares , and then demand a seat on the board . Management , fearing he would stir up trouble or that such a " barbarian " would end up on the board calling the shots , would take shareholder money to buy back his stock at a premium , just to get him to leave . This tactic is known as greenmail ; to put it bluntly , it's just collecting a fee to walk away . This guy’s name is very familiar to everyone : he's Icahn , the " Wolf King . " We’ve already covered his story in our " Corporate Raiders " series . At the end of 1984 , Pickens pulled out of Phillips Petroleum , and less than two months later , Icahn swooped in with an offer of $ 55 a share , totaling $ 8.1 billion . Where on earth was Icahn going to get that $ 8.1 billion ? That’s when we have to bring in an old acquaintance : the Junk Bond King , Michael Milken . Milken's junk bonds allowed " barbarians " to borrow as much money as they wanted . He would issue bonds for clients and hold the money in reserve before they even decided who to buy . In 1984 , the volume of these deals was $ 100 million ; two years later , it hit $ 1.2 billion . Icahn went to Milken’s firm , Drexel Burnham , but he didn't really want to borrow the money yet because the fees were just too expensive . What he wanted was for them to promise the money was available , but not to actually draw it down . The Drexel bankers came up with an idea : " We'll write a letter stating that we are highly confident in our ability to raise the funds . " Icahn turned to his lawyer and asked , " What do you think ? " The lawyer retorted bluntly , " This guy is full of hot air ! This thing has no legal standing ; what good is it ? " The meeting just kind of fizzled out . But Icahn slept on it , changed his mind the next morning , and for that one letter , Drexel charged him $ 1 million ; two months later , the same letter cost $ 3.5 million . In ten weeks , Phillips ' stock price rose from over $ 44 to over $ 50 , netting Icahn $ 52.5 million . He had also vowed , like Pickens , not to take greenmail , but $ 25 million of that $ 52.5 million was paid to him by Phillips under the guise of " expense reimbursement . " It was a very respectable-sounding label for such a payment . Just think about it : one letter , not a cent of real money , yet it triggered an $ 8.1 billion takeover bid . With such a weapon in their hands , the " barbarians " knocked on doors all over the world , and stock prices soared everywhere they went . In 1985 and 1986 , the Dow Jones rose by more than 50 percent . By January 1987 , the Dow Jones climbed for 13 consecutive days right from the start of the year , a record that was unprecedented at the time . On January 8 , the Dow Jones hit 2,000 points for the very first time . But the day it would reach its peak was still to come . On June 23 , 1987 , in New York , a small-time investment advisor made a call to the Dow Jones news agency . He claimed to represent a company offering $ 70 per share , totaling $ 6.8 billion , to acquire the parent company of Target . The agency verified his identity , and at 9:49 a.m. , the news hit the wires . Traders reacted instinctively , buying in a frenzy , forcing the exchange to halt the stock . It reopened an hour and a half later , up nine dollars . But the company he mentioned didn't even exist ; it turned out this guy had simply made up the acquisition firm himself . When reporters tracked him down , he didn't even have an office anymore . Once the news broke , his boss fired him that same morning . A reporter stood on his front lawn , asking him questions through a closed window . He said , " Acquisitions , this whole thing ? " " It's always been something you can't really see or touch . " The reporter asked , " Is this some kind of prank ? " He said no , it's more like a prank . His name was Hellinger . You can see just how crazy this market had become — from the oil fields of the early days , to a letter full of overconfidence , and finally to a phone call about a company that didn't exist . The acquisition craze had reached its end , becoming nothing more than a target for wild speculation . That same month , he sat down to run the numbers , and the result was something he couldn't even bear to imagine . In certain scenarios , if the market dropped by 3 % , LOR would have to sell futures equivalent to the entire daily trading volume of the NYSE . He later said that from the very first day he conceived of portfolio insurance , he had asked himself , " What if everyone did this ? " " I didn't like the answer I came up with , " but that year , the assets managed under portfolio insurance quadrupled . What was worse was that it gave people a false sense of security . In October 1987 , pension funds using portfolio insurance had 56 % of their money in stocks , while all pension funds averaged only 46 % . It meant that those using portfolio insurance were willing to put much more of their money into stocks . These ten points emerged because money was only put into the stock market due to the insurance ; once the market turned , this money would rush toward the same exit , following the same formula . Rubinstein , in fact , had warned about this much earlier . On September 11 , 1986 , the Dow Jones fell 4.6 % in a single day . The SEC investigated and concluded that investors ' views on the economy had changed . Rubinstein disagreed ; he believed portfolio insurance was to blame , but he couldn't provide proof . Later , a massive pension fund client approached them . They initially wanted to advise the client that there was too much money and they couldn't handle it , as the trades would crash the market . But then they thought , if we don't take it , they'll just turn to someone else . Everyone used the same strategy ; once anyone saw the snowball , no one could keep their hands off it . At the time , the entire snowball was already piled up at the peak , just waiting for a push to send it rolling down . In July 1981 , the U.S. long-term Treasury bond rate was nearly 16 % . By early 1987 , it had dropped to 7.5 % , less than half . Money became cheaper , and naturally , stocks became more attractive . However , starting in March 1987 , interest rates began to turn and head back up . The Americans had bought too many foreign goods over these past few years . The 1986 trade deficit was more than eight times that of 1981. The dollar was being sold off everywhere , hitting its lowest point against the yen since the late 1940s . To save the dollar , they had to raise interest rates . On September 4 , the Federal Reserve raised the discount rate to 6 % . Excluding the time in 1984 when they hiked and immediately pulled back , this was the first rate hike since 1981. Everyone hoped that one hike would be enough . On Sunday , October 4 , the new Fed Chairman , who had only recently taken office , appeared on a television interview . He said that while inflation wasn't here yet , if everyone believed it was coming , they would act in ways that would trigger it , and by then , they would have to use dangerously high interest rates to clean up , which would cause a severe economic contraction . This paragraph made my head spin . Translated into plain language , it meant : another rate hike is possible . This person's name was Greenspan . The way Fed Chairs speak so circuitously actually started with Greenspan . Two days later , on the morning of Tuesday , October 6 , a prominent technical analyst sent out a stock newsletter — much like the Substack subscriptions we have today — writing firmly that investors and traders should sell everything right now . He was quite famous at the time . His name is Robert Prechter , and he is the most well-known proponent of the Elliott Wave Theory , having published an investment newsletter since 1979 . In 1984 , this Elliott Wave theorist entered the U.S. Trading Championship and achieved a 44.4 % return in a supervised , real-money options account , setting a record at the time . These returns were earned in a real-money trading competition , not like the theoretical yields calculated by those armchair trading charlatans . He was one of the most popular technical analysts of the late 80s . In 1989 , FNN , the predecessor to CNBC , named him the Guru of the Decade . What’s most legendary is that he had been bullish for a long time , calling for the Dow to hit 3,600 , but turned bearish on October 6 , 1987. After the crash , his newsletter subscriptions soared to around 20,000 . On that very day , the new chairman of the U.S. SEC gave his first speech after taking office in Chicago . He was a former professor and had only started in August ; it was his first public address . While on stage , he pondered how to cool down the market and mentioned the most drastic measure of all : a total market trading halt . His name was Ruder . That day , the Dow fell 3.5 % , setting a record for the largest single-day point drop . For a market that was already jittery , the last thing it wanted to hear was the word " halt . " Ruder’s words would echo again 13 days later . Time moves to Monday , October 12. The Wall Street Journal had a long-standing stock column that , for the first time , explained portfolio insurance to average readers . The column wrote that as soon as insurance is sold , prices fall , and once prices fall , more insurance must be sold . This feedback loop could spiral into a massive rout — a concept once understood only by Ph.D.s. Now , anyone who bought the newspaper knew it . The next day , Tuesday , the market rose slightly by 1.5 % . Then , 93 minutes after the close , Democrats on the House Ways and Means Committee held a closed-door meeting to agree on a package of tax hikes . Several provisions aimed at the interest payments on debt-funded acquisitions by " barbarians , " and proposed a 50 % tax on greenmail payments . The committee claimed that hostile takeovers harmed the economy , employees , and communities ; they didn't mention shareholders once . The hand propping up the stock market let go . October 14th , Wednesday , brought more bad news . The August trade deficit hit $ 15.7 billion , $ 1.5 billion higher than expected , and long-term interest rates crossed 10 % for the first time in two years . That day , the Dow Jones fell 3.8 % , breaking the record set just last week . Stocks waiting for " barbarians at the gate " fell even harder , by half as much again . On Thursday , it dropped another 2.4 % , and on Friday , it fell 108 points — the first time it had dropped over 100 points in a single day . As the closing bell approached , a maintenance worker at the exchange climbed up to watch the bell , determined to make it ring on time ; it hadn't rung properly the past few days , and no one wanted this day to drag on for even a second longer . In three days , the Dow Jones had plunged 10.4 % , marking the worst three-day stretch since the German invasion of France in May 1940 . But that wasn't the scariest part . According to the portfolio insurance model , they should have sold at least $ 12 billion in futures over those three days . How much did they actually sell ? Less than $ 4 billion . $ 8 billion was still hanging in the balance , and this was no secret . Anyone with a calculator could run the numbers and compare them with the futures trading volume to realize that when the market opened on Monday , $ 8 billion more would be waiting to be sold — more than the entire daily volume of the New York Stock Exchange . The math was clear ; now the whole market could calculate it . Over the weekend , an institution using portfolio insurance received notice that on Monday , they had to sell off 70 % of their remaining stocks . On that Friday , a missile fired from an Iranian-controlled peninsula struck a U.S. - flagged tanker in Kuwaiti waters . Early Monday morning , four U.S. warships opened fire , shelling two Iranian offshore platforms . The example mentioned back then — the locations all lined up ; the hand holding the weight let go , and the hand pushing down was seen by the entire world . Monday , October 16th . The NYSE had an electronic order system where brokers could input orders to be sent directly to the trading floor . That morning , the exchange opened an hour early , as if to let everyone place orders calmly , but the result was that orders flooded in at four or five times the usual volume , and almost all of them were to sell . When the 9:30 bell rang , 14 million shares in sell orders had already piled up in the system , amounting to about $ 500 million . By 9:45 , the Dow Jones had only fallen 21 points , but don't celebrate too early — many stocks couldn't even open . There were too many sellers and too few buyers , and the stocks that hadn't opened were still quoted at Friday's old prices . By 10:30 , giants like Exxon and Sears hadn't traded a single share . Eight of the 30 Dow components hadn't even opened . Even with stale prices filling in the gaps , the Dow was already down 104 points . At this moment , the New York Stock Exchange chairman’s office was packed with Wall Street bigwigs , including the heads of several major firms . The chairman had started as a clerk at his father's brokerage at 16 , hated it , joined the Marines , served as a sergeant in the Korean War , then returned to climb his way to the top of the exchange . He actually had quite a connection to China ; he had visited Beijing and met Deng Xiaoping . Deng gave him a share of Shanghai Feiyue Acoustics , the first publicly issued stock in the new China . The man’s name was John Phelan . He knew clearly that only the President or the exchange itself had the authority to close the market ; even the SEC didn't have that power . If the market situation turned out worse than Friday , the calls for a shutdown would certainly grow louder . He didn't issue an order ; instead , he sought a consensus from everyone in the room : no matter how bad it got , the NYSE would not close today . At 10:45 , IBM finally opened , plunging 7.4 % . In the first hour and a half , the Dow had fallen 208 points , twice as much as the entire day on Friday . Then , surprisingly , the market stabilized . Speculators who couldn't open positions in New York scrambled to cover , effectively short-covering , and nearly half the losses were recovered in half an hour . Just then , in a Washington hotel , SEC Chairman David Ruder had just finished speaking when reporters surrounded him with the same question : would trading be suspended today ? He mentioned that he had spoken with Phelan and that he had indeed considered the possibility of a suspension . The ghosts of the previous day returned . Eleven minutes later , wire services ran the headline : " SEC has discussed suspension ; none planned for now . " Thirteen minutes later , another message came : " Ruder discusses suspension ; everything is possible . " The market didn't wait for the second update . As soon as the word " suspension " appeared , the sell orders came flooding back in . Here , I should explain : back then in Chicago , when futures got cheap , people would buy the futures at a low price , then immediately sell the corresponding basket of stocks in New York to pocket the difference — this is called index arbitrage . It’s like someone paying you $ 97 to take a $ 100 bill . With this buying and selling , the selling pressure in Chicago was transmitted to New York in the blink of an eye . Between 11:30 and 1:30 , portfolio insurance sold another 10,000 futures contracts worth $ 1.3 billion , and the arbitrageurs who took the other side immediately dumped stocks in New York . Another portfolio insurance firm offloaded $ 400 million in spot stocks directly . Leland's assumption that there would always be a " smart buyer " waiting to catch whatever you sell completely collapsed at that very moment . These buy orders , which usually seemed so robust , vanished in the blink of an eye when everyone tried to sell at once ; liquidity had dried up . The trader mentioned earlier held back the model that day ; when the model signaled to sell , he refused , fearing that selling everything would crash the market . At 12:55 , the Dow Jones plummeted another 194 points . Just then , an editor at Dow Jones News Service re-sent a piece of news from Rudy , without clarifying that it was outdated . I don't know if it was intentional to coordinate with the bears to drive the market down further , much like how people on Twitter spread fake news about Trump reaching a deal with Iran to manipulate oil prices ; it had the same effect . Everyone thought the market would be suspended again , so they rushed to trade before the close . From 1:15 to 2:05 , the Dow fell below 2,000 points for the first time since January . It dropped another 300 points in the final 75 minutes . At 4:00 , the closing bell rang ; the Dow Jones had plunged a full 508 points , or 22.6 % . The selling didn't stop because they were done , but because the time was up . Market makers were absorbing the selling pressure all day long . They were holding $ 1.5 billion in stocks , ten times the usual amount . One out of every four firms hit the exchange's capital limit , while the portfolio insurers sold all day but still couldn't keep pace with their models . At this point , Vran held a press conference for 50 reporters , and one asked if this counted as a panic . He refused to admit it . He claimed that market makers were doing fine . In reality , many were not fine at all and were on the verge of liquidation . He spoke slowly , answering every question while trying his best not to use jargon . A Wall Street Journal reporter later recalled that he captivated the entire press conference ; it might have been the only bright spot in the US stock market that day . But the true drama of that night wasn't in New York — it was in Chicago . The chairman of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange was a man named Melamed . He was born in Poland , and as a child , to escape the Nazis , he fled with his parents through Lithuania and Japan before reaching the United States . In the 1960s , while in law school in Chicago , he looked for a law firm job . He saw a company name with a string of surnames , thinking it must be a law firm , and applied . They hired him , but it turned out to be Merrill Lynch . He ended up working as a runner on the futures floor — you have to admit , he was pretty bold to be so casual about job hunting . Twenty years later , he became the chairman of that exchange . When Melamed first heard about portfolio insurance , he laughed and called it a fabrication . Traders know this : when you truly need buying support , it’s the first thing to disappear . On Monday night , he stayed at the exchange all night long . There is a rule in futures : losers must deliver their payments to the clearinghouse that same night , while winners get their money the next day . Usually , about 250 million dollars flows through daily , but that night it was 2.5 billion . The rules are strict ; if the money isn't there , the exchange doesn't open the next day . Greenspan called him that night with just one question : " Can you open tomorrow ? " Melamed replied , " I don't know , some big companies owe a billion and are dragging their feet on paying . " At 2:00 AM , Melamed called the company's chairman directly . He later recalled how absurd it was — a stranger calling from Chicago at 2:00 AM saying , " You owe us a billion dollars , we want our money . " The funds trickled in , but by 8:00 AM , with only 20 minutes left before the market opened , the clearing account was still short by 100 million dollars . The account was with a banker at Continental Illinois named Wilmer . Wilmer said , " We have 2.4 billion , but we're still 100 million short . It doesn't look like it's coming . " Before the opening bell , the Fed had already declared it was ready to provide liquidity to the economy and financial system at any time . The President of the New York Fed called the major banks , saying , " We are right here , take whatever you need . " It is clear that the Fed's " too big to fail " doctrine was already in place back then . Before the market opened on Tuesday , the NYSE announced , " Regarding program trading , please refrain from using electronic systems to place orders . " Though it was called a request , everyone could hear it was actually a warning . The link between Chicago and New York was effectively severed just like that . Specialists were still sitting on 1.5 billion in stock they were desperate to offload ; opening prices kept hitting new highs , with Coca-Cola opening at Friday's level , as if Monday never happened . At 10:27 AM , the Dow Jones rebounded by nearly 200 points . But after just an hour , portfolio insurers returned to the sell side . Futures dropped to 19 % cheaper than the stocks ; it was like being able to grab $ 100 worth of shares for just $ 81 . Seeing this , the traders in New York kept selling at those prices ; the specialists couldn't hold the line anymore , and trading grew increasingly thin . Fhelan later said the scariest part was the vacuum ; it looked like it was about to drop another 500 points , and there wasn't a single buy order below . At 11:31 AM , ten out of the thirty Dow Jones stocks were halted . At 12:15 PM , a worker at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange walked into the S & P futures trading pit with a megaphone and announced the suspension of trading . At 12:21 PM , the Dow Jones had fallen to 1616 points , losing 40 % of its value in 56 days since its August high . Fhelan called the White House Chief of Staff , who told him , " Do whatever you have to do ; we fully support you . " After hanging up , the Chief of Staff had White House legal counsel draft documents , preparing for the President to order the exchange closed as a backup measure . At 12:30 PM , the specialists begged Fhelan to shut down , but he wouldn't budge : " If we close it , it will never open again . " At 12:38 PM , at another Chicago exchange , there was an obscure contract tracking 20 blue-chip stocks that no one usually touched ; suddenly , someone bought 808 lots at once , totaling $ 60 million . Within those two minutes , the price of this contract surged above the current prices of those 20 stocks , and the arbitrage direction instantly flipped . People began selling contracts to buy stocks , and buy orders in New York started to surge . Investment banks weren't sitting idle either ; First Boston alone called 200 corporate clients , urging them to buy back their own shares . Over Monday and Tuesday , U.S. companies announced a total of $ 6.2 billion in buybacks . At 1:04 , Chicago reopened for the close , and the Dow rose by 102.5.9 % . In a market that had evaporated by $ 1 trillion in a week , who was it that bought those 808 lots that brought back $ 60 million at the end ? Was someone testing the waters on purpose ? Did the " national team " step in ? Senate investigators suspected it , and Wall Street Journal reporters suspected it too , but to this day , there is no definitive conclusion . Time moved to 3:00 AM on Wednesday , and a firm that had operated for 60 years — a veteran institution that survived 1929 and the Great Depression — couldn't hold on any longer , so Merrill Lynch acquired it . The market crash effectively ended here , and portfolio insurance was thoroughly discredited . A week after the crash , the Chairman of the House Ways and Means Committee softened his stance on January 29th ; he said he could accept a reasonable compromise , and the tax-hike proposal that nearly killed the " barbarians " began to waver . The Dow hit a new high in August 1989 , just one day shy of the previous peak — exactly two years later . Looking back , the " barbarians " had pushed stock prices too high , portfolio insurance was selling frantically , interest rates were rising , the dollar was falling , and Congress slashed buyouts — several forces collided in the same week . After 1987 , Americans also installed circuit breakers on the stock market . You see , after every crash , everyone wants to find a mechanism to lock it down , but the problems they solve are always the ones that happened the last time . Leland actually knew this day would come ; he had said before that if everyone did this , he wouldn't like the answer he came up with . But the pay was just too good , and faced with such financial temptation , he couldn't maintain his discipline and turn down so much money . The same thing happened again later in 2010 , when a hedging program mistook active trading for someone taking over the market , and the U.S. stock market flashed crashed in the blink of an eye . There is also a foreshadowing : when Milken sold junk bonds back then , he had a narrative — the downside risks were already priced in , making them a better deal . Furthermore , by packaging these bonds into a portfolio , the risk was actually lower . Why was that ? Because these debts are relatively independent entities and it’s very rare for all companies to go bankrupt at once , buying such a portfolio fund is actually a very profitable business . Twenty years later , Wall Street used the same pitch to sell another type of debt , one backed by millions of homes .
