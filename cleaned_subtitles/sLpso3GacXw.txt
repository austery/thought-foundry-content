First , let me show you an image . In northern Indiana in the U.S. , there’s a small place called New Carlisle where a data center campus is being built . It’s funded by Amazon specifically for Anthropic , the company that builds large models — you know , the one backing Claude . The plan calls for 32 buildings , clearly marked on the map ; buildings 17 and 18 just got their roofs on , with generators and cooling units still being installed . Looking further ahead , from number 19 to 32 , it’s just a leveled patch of land where you can't even see the foundations . This map was made by a research organization called AppleAI , which specializes in one specific task . Using satellite imagery and drone footage from above to track data centers — if you click through their website , the more you look , the more uneasy you get : so much money has been dumped in , but where are the buildings ? This isn't an isolated case ; Morgan Stanley calculated this year that more than half of the GPUs sold between 2026 and 2028 might not have a place to plug in . The Financial Times even wrote an article about this , with a title that asks : " So , where are all the data centers ? " Investment bank Jefferies also surveyed these sites , finding that of the data centers originally planned for 2026 , totaling 24 gigawatts , only half — or 12 gigawatts — have actually broken ground . What is the concept of a gigawatt ? One gigawatt is roughly the output of a single nuclear power unit ; 12 gigawatts of data centers is essentially equivalent to saying you need to build 12 nuclear reactors . So many of these projects are still just sitting on paper , with construction yet to begin . In late September , Morgan Stanley raised its estimate of the U.S. data center power gap before 2028 to 33 gigawatts , meaning the electricity for over 30 nuclear reactors is still unaccounted for . Just imagine this scenario : the appliances have already been delivered , and refrigerators and air conditioners are piling up , but the building isn't powered yet ; chips have already been manufactured , just sitting there gathering dust , waiting for power . But the problem is , a huge portion of the money used to buy these chips and build these structures is borrowed . The interest on that borrowed money accrues every single day , and for every day the data center stays unpowered , revenue is delayed by another day . So the question is , just how big is this bill , really ? As it turns out , in September of this year , someone actually sat down and crunched the numbers . Chips are waiting for people , but interest rates wait for no one . Just two weeks ago , Oracle sent an email to its data center suppliers , stating that due to force majeure , they might need to reduce their rent payments . In fact , this is because power shortages have prevented many data centers under construction from coming online , so Oracle is clearly looking to ease its financial pressure . I first heard about this paper on an American finance podcast , where the two hosts spent over half an hour constantly insisting that everyone must read it , so I decided to look it up . This paper comes from the Brookings Institution , a long-established Washington think tank that hosts economics conferences every spring and fall . It holds significant weight in the U.S. economic scene ; this year's autumn session saw the paper posted online on September 23rd , followed by discussions on the 24th and 25th . The author is a real estate professor at Columbia Business School named Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh . His full name is quite difficult to pronounce , so you can see the English spelling I've provided in the comments section . You might ask , why is someone who teaches real estate weighing in on AI matters ? The connection is actually quite strong ; people always view AI as some invisible , intangible revolution . Just think , these large models all reside on cloud service servers , but right now , the physical footprint they leave behind is made entirely of steel , concrete , cables , and chips . Actually , while AI sounds like a software business , on the balance sheet , it is a construction business . The methodology is quite solid . He utilized a database that tracks data center projects ; as of July 23rd , there were 2,933 projects across the U.S. either operational or planned , totaling 566 gigawatts . By the end of 2024 , the total U.S. data center capacity was only 39 gigawatts , so you see , the total registered in this database is more than 14 times the current stock . Developer brochures are always prone to exaggeration , but this professor didn't buy the hype ; like an auditor , he checked every single project one by one . The largest chunk consists of projects without even a completion year , totaling 353 gigawatts , or two-thirds of all plans post - 2024 . He judges that most won't be built , and for those without even announced capacity , I’ve applied an even steeper discount . After these cuts , nearly 227 gigawatts , in his estimation , will never be built . Essentially , while these numbers were announced , in reality , 117 gigawatts will be pushed off until after 2032 . Only 183 gigawatts can truly be realized before 2032. In other words , out of all these plans emerging after 2024 , the professor believes only about one-third will survive . That is a pretty aggressive discount , isn't it ? How much is 183 gigawatts ? The Three Gorges Dam has an installed capacity of 22.5 gigawatts . 183 gigawatts is roughly the size of eight Three Gorges Dams . This is already a heavily discounted figure , so based on this one-third expectation , how much would it cost ? The answer is 10.3 trillion US dollars over the eight-year period from 2025 to 2032 . The professor first calculated a unit price : one gigawatt of AI data center capacity , including power and chips , is about 41 billion US dollars , nearly 280 billion RMB . This number is so massive that simply reading it out offers no real sense of scale . If you convert it at current exchange rates , it’s about 70 trillion RMB . China's GDP last year was 140 trillion , so this round of AI infrastructure in the US would consume half of China's annual economic output over eight years . The professor also did something quite clever : instead of comparing absolute amounts , he compared them as a percentage of the GDP at that time . This way , projects from 200 years ago and today can be measured on the same scale . He dug up five major infrastructure eras in US history . From 1836 to 1841 , during the canal-building era — when the US still relied on horse-drawn barges — annual investment was less than 0.7 % of GDP . From 1870 to 1890 , the US built railways . This was the craziest period in American history , with an average annual investment of 2.24 % of GDP . From 1870 to 1881 , it spiked over 4 % . That was when transcontinental rails blanketed the West . Back then , it was as if the whole country had caught a fever . The latter three — early 20 th-century electrification , the 1950s interstate highway system , and the late 90s telecom fiber-optic build-out — hovered around 0.5 % to just over 1.1 % of GDP . In this AI cycle , the average annual investment of 3.63 % of GDP has already surpassed that of the railway construction era . Just think , building railways was a project that stitched an entire continent together , yet AI spends 60 % more per year than that era did . Looking closer , this spending isn't even spread out ; in 2026 , it hit 4.5 % of GDP , or $ 1.43 trillion , which is already more than America's annual military budget — and that’s just for one year . By 2032 , it’s set to hit 5.1 % . What’s worse is that this $ 10.3 trillion only accounts for the initial building construction and hardware installation . Future chip replacement costs haven't even been factored in yet , which leads to a very critical question . Who exactly is paying for all this money ? The answer lies right here in this chart before your eyes . We’ve grouped together the five largest cloud companies in the U.S .: Meta , Microsoft , Amazon , Oracle , and Google . The chart shows two lines : one is their annual spending on data centers and chips , and the other is the cash they bring in from day-to-day operations , known as operating cash flow . In 2020 , these five companies spent a combined $ 96.8 billion ; by 2025 , that figure reached $ 415.8 billion . For 2026 , based on company plans and Wall Street forecasts , it will hit $ 805 billion — that’s roughly 5.4 trillion RMB . So , how much can these five companies actually earn ? The operating cash flow is projected to be $ 707.1 billion . This is the spending ; the amount invested is now exceeding the money they make . Look at where these two lines intersect — specifically , this ratio has exceeded 100 % . This means your capital expenditure can no longer be covered by operating cash flow alone ; you must take on massive debt . The most obvious one here is actually Oracle ; you can see their planned spending is far higher than their free cash flow , which is why Oracle’s debt situation is the most severe among them . And there’s an interesting detail here : this $ 805 billion doesn't include the data centers they rent , which must be calculated separately . Think about it : back in 2020 , during the pandemic , for every $ 100 these companies earned , they spent less than $ 40 on data centers — they were quite comfortable . Now , $ 100 isn't enough ; you have to borrow a dozen more . Think of it like an ordinary household . In the past , you'd use your annual bonus for home renovations . No matter how luxurious you made it , you were spending money you earned . If the bonus isn't enough now , you have to take out loans or use credit cards to pay for it . Everyone can do that math , right ? In that podcast , I really resonated with what the host said : when you spend your own money , it doesn't matter if you finish late ; at worst , you just make a bit less . But once you start borrowing , the clock starts ticking . It’s just like a mortgage , isn’t it ? Regardless of whether you’re making money or not , when that monthly payment date comes , the bank doesn't care if you've moved in or not . So , who’s going to cover that gap ? According to Goldman Sachs estimates , between 2025 and 2028 , cloud giants will need to spend about $ 2.9 trillion to expand computing power , and over half of that will have to come from borrowed money . The largest chunk of that borrowed portion , about $ 800 billion , comes from private credit — funds outside the banking system that specialize in lending . A huge portion of their capital comes from pension funds and insurance companies . Why are these institutions willing to lend ? It’s actually quite simple : the interest is higher than government bonds , and they are chasing stable , high returns . The next layer down is even more interesting . Companies like OpenAI and Anthropic , which build large models , don't have the credit to support such massive borrowing . They have to rely on the cloud giants ' balance sheets , borrowing against the cloud giants ' credit to get that computing power . Simply put , these " little brothers " don't have enough credit limit on their own cards , so they have to swipe their " big brother’s " card . The clock is already ticking . So , what exactly are they buying with this borrowed money ? The professor took a standard 200 - megawatt AI data center and broke down the ledger for everyone to see . The total price tag is $ 8.2 billion . The building , substation , and cooling systems add up to about one-third . The remaining two-thirds — or to be precise , 68 % — consist of the chips and servers in the racks . The bulk of the money is all tied up in the chips . So , how long can these chips actually last ? The professor gave an estimate of 6 years based on current industry and accounting standards , while the building and power infrastructure are estimated at 20 years . However , he wrote in the report that while GPUs might last three to six years , they are already economically obsolete by then . I think this is actually the biggest difference between this current AI building spree and the railroad mania of the past . Just think about it : the phone you bought six years ago is probably sitting in a drawer gathering dust right now , assuming you didn't sell it as a used device . Take the Beijing-Zhangjiakou Railway built by Zhan Tianyou back in the day , or that chevron-shaped track at Qinglongqiao — it's been over 100 years . Even today , suburban trains are still running on those very tracks . You build a railway once and it lasts a century , though it needs maintenance , but you install a chip and it lasts six or seven years at most , maybe only three . But what about the debt behind these data centers ? There was a star deal in the U.S. data center industry : a bond issued in October 2025 , the largest investment-grade corporate bond in U.S. history , maturing in 2049 . The professor added a reminder in the appendix : chips installed in 2025 or 2026 will need a major overhaul by around 2030 , but think about it , what do these buildings rely on for rent ? They rely on the chips inside still being able to make money for the tenants . Data centers designed for today's generation of chips might be obsolete before the building is even finished . As the power density of machines grows , cooling requirements mean costly renovations are needed every single time . If you look at these years together — replacing chips around 2030 , but not paying off debt until 2049 — the debt is long-term , while the economic life of the underlying assets is much shorter . So , data center builders face a core problem , one that all debt investors encounter : if your asset has a six-year lifespan but you have to pay off the debt over 24 years , can this massive construction boom really be sustained ? You really need to look at three things : how fast revenue grows , whether pricing holds , and exactly how many years a chip can last . For the first calculation , the professor asks a simple question : with $ 10.3 trillion invested , how much must be recovered annually for the investors not to lose money ? He set a threshold : earning 10 % a year , without leverage , just investing their own capital to make a 10 % return . This requirement is not greedy at all by Wall Street standards . He made a generous assumption : for every $ 100 in revenue , $ 50 in cash remains , which is already a very healthy profit margin . Calculations show that by 2032 , these data centers would need to pull in $ 3.7 trillion a year . You might not have a concept of this number , but as a share of U.S. GDP , it would account for 9.2 % that year — just think about that . Think about it , US healthcare costs account for 10 % of its GDP , yet the AI industry is expected to generate over half of that amount in revenue ! Let’s look at railroads ; during the railway era , the US spent 2 % of its GDP annually on construction . This time , we need to recoup 9 % of GDP every year to break even . So , who is currently collecting this money ? The professor provided a benchmark : OpenAI and Anthropic combined bring in about $ 100 billion annually . How fast would they need to grow to reach $ 3.7 trillion ? They would need 80 % annual growth until 2032. To put that in perspective , imagine a young person earning 10,000 a month with an 80 % raise every year for six years . What would their salary be in the sixth year ? 340,000 . I wonder if anyone around you fits that description ? Personally , I’ve never seen anyone like that . This is just the professor's middle-ground estimate . He made a table adjusting return requirements and profit margins ; even in the most optimistic scenario with a 5 % return , you still need $ 2.3 trillion in annual revenue . In the most pessimistic scenario , you’d need $ 6.7 trillion . The gap between the two is nearly threefold . The professor is very restrained in his assessment , noting that compared to today's compute prices , this revenue threshold doesn't seem outrageous , but he adds a caveat : your prices must hold firm . But the question is , can these prices actually hold ? The professor spread that $ 3.7 trillion across every chip . With 183 gigawatts of power , based on Nvidia's current mainstream server racks , you could fit about 77 million GPUs . Each GPU would need to charge $ 5.50 per hour for the books to balance . And that's assuming full load without a second of downtime — a luxury that doesn't exist in reality . At 80 % utilization , the price would need to rise to $ 6.90 ; at 70 % , it would have to climb to $ 7.90 . So , how much does it cost to rent a top-tier GPU on the market right now ? The professor checked , and the hourly rental rate is roughly between $ 6 and just over $ 10 . Keep in mind , this is the on-demand rental price . For long-term contracts or spot-market deals , the actual transaction price is even lower . So he says , at today's prices , the math works out . But then he immediately asks : once all 183 gigawatts are built , will there be enough demand , and can prices hold at today's levels ? Coincidentally , just one day before this paper was posted , on September 22nd , the U.S. AI industry experienced something . Google released its new flagship model , priced 20 % cheaper than the previous generation , and claims it saves 40 % in actual operation . 90 minutes later , OpenAI launched two new models , cutting prices directly in half . This wasn't the first time ; on July 30th , OpenAI had already slashed the price of a budget model by 80 % . US tech media reported in August that the desperate price cuts from both companies were due to Chinese open-source models , which are 60 % to 90 % cheaper per token , causing customers to move in that direction . A token can be understood as the unit for which AI charges based on the work it performs . Chinese audiences are very familiar with this scene . In May 2024 , DeepSeek dropped large model prices to one yuan per million tokens , with ByteDance , Alibaba , and Baidu following with round after round of cuts , some even making them free . Everyone remembers the large model price war that year . Of course , there is still a layer between model price cuts and GPU rental price cuts , but the logic is already very clear . If what you sell to end-users becomes cheaper and cheaper , the rental costs of your upstream data centers will inevitably be forced down as well . If everyone is going to fight a price war to grab customers at the model level , it will surely cut into GPU rental costs . The remaining bill , then , is the cost of replacing chips mentioned earlier . Let me share an analogy that is easy to understand . Everyone has been to an internet café , right ? My childhood dream was to open my own internet café so I could play games as much as I wanted . But I later realized you don't actually have to open an internet café to play ; you just need to have enough money to pay the hourly rate . Those who have run an internet café know that renovations , furniture , and network cables last for years . But the machines are different ; if you don't replace your computers every few years , they can't run new games , and customers will just turn around and go next door . AI data centers are essentially giant internet cafes ; the building fit-out accounts for about 30 % , while the chips , acting as the computers , make up nearly 70 % . In his paper , the professor estimated a six-year lifespan for these chips , but he also ran a stress test : what if the chips only last three years ? The result was that for every $ 100 invested , the annual cost that needs to be recouped jumps from over $ 19 to $ 31 . The annual revenue threshold jumps from $ 3.7 trillion to $ 6 trillion . What does $ 6 trillion actually mean ? It is equivalent to 14.8 % of the projected U.S. GDP in 2032. For each GPU , the hourly rental price would jump directly from $ 5.50 to $ 8.80 , landing in the upper tier of today's rental price range , which is the more expensive bracket . So , do you see where the problem lies ? Historically , massive infrastructure projects like canals , railways , or fiber optics could be used for many years ; even if the railway company went bankrupt , the tracks remained . For the same dollar of investment , the assets left behind by AI are far less valuable than those old-school projects . In plain terms , the money is spent and the building remains , but the most valuable things inside will turn into electronic waste after six years . The thing is , borrowing money to buy chips now involves using the chips themselves as collateral . The professional rating agency Moody's previously warned that chips as collateral are different from houses or airplanes ; they become obsolete too quickly , so who knows what they'll be worth second-hand ? Nobody can say for sure . This August , six major Wall Street institutions set up a $ 500 billion loan pool specifically to lend money to people buying Nvidia chips . Nvidia stepped in to provide a floor , guaranteeing 25 % of the chips ' residual value , up to $ 125 billion . The chip seller is essentially guaranteeing the resale price of its own product , so you see , Nvidia is stepping in to keep this circular financing framework going . They don't want the AI infrastructure wave to just fizzle out . These calculations remain on paper , but there are several walls between the paper and actual implementation . The first wall is electricity . Columbia professors calculated that a 200 - megawatt data center consumes as much power in a year as 170,000 U.S. households . One data center is equivalent to the power consumption of a small city , and building out the entire 183 gigawatts would be like creating an entirely new power grid on top of all existing U.S. residential usage . So , where will all this electricity come from ? Morgan Stanley says that in some parts of the U.S. , data centers have to wait five to seven years just to get connected to the power grid . The 33 - gigawatt deficit I mentioned earlier is the gap that remains even after accounting for the data centers ' own power plants and onsite gas turbines . As we’ve discussed in previous episodes , for that data center in New Mexico , the state couldn’t approve the natural gas pipeline , and Google immediately issued a force majeure notice . Another bottleneck is even more absurd ; once these chips are manufactured , they can’t be used directly — they must go through an advanced packaging process to solder the compute chip to the memory . Ninety percent of the world’s advanced packaging for AI chips is controlled by TSMC . TSMC has a factory in Arizona , USA , where the wafers are produced , but after they are made , they have to be loaded onto a plane , flown to Taiwan for packaging , and then shipped back to the U.S. TSMC is building its own packaging line in Arizona with a target to start production by 2029 , meaning for the next few years , most US-made AI chips must still cross the Pacific twice . When I heard that podcast host , he said it sounds like a joke , and that what might eventually crash this feast is the packaging phase , not to mention transformers and cooling equipment , which are all stuck in queues . Just imagine this scenario : Wall Street’s orders are calculated by the quarter , the power grid on the ground is on a multi-year timeline , and the borrowed money accrues interest by the day . You see , these three clocks are all running at different speeds . In October 2025 , the US bond market closed a deal that a Columbia professor later wrote about in a paper , using it as a case study for the entire AI financing structure . The main player is Meta , our Facebook company , which has a data center campus in Louisiana called Hyper , with a two-gigawatt capacity and a total investment of about $ 30 billion . That $ 30 billion only covers the building and power ; Meta , as the tenant , pays for the chips itself , and Meta initially paid to build the facility itself . Later , they made a move to sell an 80 % stake in the project to private equity giant Blackstone for $ 2.5 billion . The two partnered to form a new company with a rather interesting name : Beignet , named after those New Orleans French donuts covered in powdered sugar — these American desserts are really inedible , they’re just too sweet . The first thing this " donut company " did was borrow money , issuing $ 27 billion in debt all at once . S & P gave it an A + rating , just one notch below Meta itself ; this is the largest single investment-grade corporate bond issue in U.S. history . If you look at these numbers together , the building is only worth 30 billion , but they borrowed 27 billion — effectively 90 % debt . The professor calculated an indicator called the debt service coverage ratio at 1.12 x ; in plain terms , for every $ 1.12 the project brings in , $ 1.00 must go toward interest and principal . That leaves a margin of just 12 cents — is that really enough to cover Meta's own books ? By the end of 2025 , its debt-to-equity ratio will be 25 % on paper , but only 4 % by market cap ; it looks spotless . Now look at the lease : Meta is renting , not buying , starting in 2029 with four-year renewals until 2049 , maturing the same year as the bonds . Every time a renewal comes up , Meta could technically walk away , but doing so comes at a cost : the data center must be sold , and if the proceeds fall short of the contract's floor price , Meta pays the difference . Anyone in finance understands this arrangement immediately ; it’s a lease in name , but debt in substance . So , what is the cost ? The " donut shop's " bond interest rate is 6.58 % , which is at least a full percentage point more expensive than if Meta borrowed the money directly . Over the entire loan term , the extra interest paid exceeds $ 5 billion ; you’re probably wondering why Meta would operate this way . Actually , Meta is doing this to make its financial statements look as clean as possible . In July , Meta worked with Bedrock again in Texas to set up another company , this one named " Sopaipilla , " issuing $ 12.3 billion in debt ; it’s like Meta is turning these " dessert shops " into a chain . You might wonder why Meta would adopt such a structure to borrow money . Especially when it means paying all that extra interest . In fact , under current U.S. accounting standards , Meta doesn't have to record a single cent of this lease or that price guarantee on its balance sheet until they actually take effect . Just think about it : based on the contract design , one of these two things is bound to materialize in the future , yet currently , you won't see any impact on the balance sheet . I've calculated the industry total ; cloud giants have about $ 970 billion in lease commitments , and $ 660 billion of that isn't on the books at all because the leases haven't started yet . The Wall Street Journal's calculations are even bolder : the off-balance-sheet liabilities for Microsoft , Google , Amazon , and Meta combined total $ 1.4 trillion . $ 904 billion of that is future rent , and $ 1.52 trillion is future purchase commitments , with the bulk going toward buying chips . Only $ 604 billion is on the books , so just imagine — of the $ 3 trillion in liabilities related to data centers , only one-fifth is actually recorded on their balance sheets . Even more troubling is the lack of transparency . Professors point out that rated , publicly reported debt only makes up a small fraction of data center financing ; the larger share comes from syndicated loans and private credit , where almost no information is available externally . Where does this private credit money even come from ? As I’ve mentioned in previous segments , it’s money from pension funds and insurance companies . Just think about how long that chain is . The professor also highlighted a detail : if computing demand ever falls short of expectations , these cloud giants will move workloads back to their own buildings to ensure their own facility utilization , leaving rented space empty — shifting the burden onto those external landlords . There’s also an interesting signal : these publicly traded data center REITs , which specialize in collecting rent , used to have a correlation coefficient with the broader market of around 0.5 , acting as a safe haven . Over the last two years , that’s risen to about 1.0 ; they now rise and fall in lockstep with the stock market . What used to be a reliable " iron rice bowl " of rental income has now become just another plank on the AI ship . From the looks of it , off-balance-sheet financing itself doesn't necessarily increase risk , but the problem is that before something goes wrong , you simply cannot see these interconnected risks from the outside . You can calculate the debt you can see , but for the debt you can't see , you can only guess . With borrowing happening both off and on the books , there is only one word to describe the cloud giants ' actions in the bond market this year : gobbling up . This February , Google’s parent company borrowed about $ 31.5 billion in just two days across the US dollar , British pound , and Swiss franc markets . One of those deals even stunned London's financial district : a 100 - year bond denominated in pounds , maturing in 2126 , with an interest rate just over 6 % . They wanted to borrow £ 1 billion , but received £ 9.5 billion in orders . The last time a tech company issued a century bond was Motorola in 1997. That was right when the internet was just starting its climb to the top . We all know what eventually happened to Motorola , right ? If even cash-rich companies like Google are starting to stock up , what are others supposed to do ? Since the beginning of this year , a few tech giants have issued over 180 billion dollars in USD-denominated bonds alone . A London-based bond fund calculated that their 30 - year bond issues are competing for the same pool of investors — pension funds and insurers — as U.S. Treasury 30 - year bonds . The New York Times published an article in late September stating that cloud giants are rushing to borrow long-term funds , crowding out other companies that are forced to borrow short-term , having to refinance every three to five years ; if interest rates keep rising , each refinance becomes a burden . Moreover , the weakest link among these cloud giants is already starting to shake ; on July 9th , S & P downgraded Oracle to BB - , just one notch away from junk status . Oracle has about 117 billion dollars in outstanding debt , ranking second among non-financial firms in the U.S. investment-grade corporate bond index . Let’s imagine this : according to the index rules , if any two of the three major rating agencies downgrade Oracle to junk status , it will have to be removed from this investment-grade index . Funds worldwide that only buy investment-grade assets would all receive the same instruction on the same day : sell . But sell to whom , and at what price ? Wall Street has a name for companies in Oracle's situation : " fallen angels , " meaning they’ve been demoted from angel to mortal , with their market value taking an immediate hit . Based on credit analysis , this is a medium-term risk , not an immediate one , but this risk is growing larger by the day . " I was watching a podcast where the two hosts live in a small mountain town in Colorado , which has a narrow-gauge railway that has been running for over 100 years and is still in operation today . One host , describing the train that carries tourists to see the scenery , said , in essence , that during the railway boom , many rail companies went bankrupt , and those who bought the stocks and bonds lost everything , but the tracks remained ; someone always took over and used the hundreds of thousands of miles of track . The professor looked back at history and noted that from railroads to telecommunications , major infrastructure booms usually run into trouble for three reasons : demand fails to keep pace , borrowing becomes difficult , or the pace of new technology rollout doesn't match the maturity of the debt . To be fair , this AI wave will also leave something behind . For instance , buildings , grid connections , and substations are built to last at least 20 years , but the chips , which account for 86 % of the cost , will be obsolete in five or six years , forcing you to buy them all over again . To the hosts , that sounds absolutely insane . This image reminds me of the bike-sharing competition in China a while back , right ? In just over a year , dozens of companies dumped more than 20 million bikes onto the streets , only to go bust one by one later on . There is another point the hosts made quite well : the US Treasury Secretary keeps saying the US economy needs to hit 3 % growth , and money spent on AI infrastructure counts toward that year's GDP . Research from the Federal Reserve also indicates that from 2025 to the first quarter of this year , AI infrastructure has made a significant contribution to US economic growth . The problem is , while the borrowed money spent counts as GDP , the debt is still there ; so once this spending spree ends , what will actually be left in our hands ? The railroad era left behind tracks , but this round might leave behind a graveyard of chips ; to the two podcast hosts , if this AI infrastructure bubble bursts , it would be far worse than the 2008 financial crisis . However , in the professor's view , it is still too early to conclude that AI poses a systemic risk . These problems do not mean that a crisis is imminent . If demand remains strong , data centers stay full , and models continue to improve , this infrastructure investment could very well hold up . What he is truly concerned about is under what conditions the defaults of one project could cascade and spread throughout the entire financial system . At the end of the paper , the professor lists a new piece he and his collaborators are publishing this November , titled " The Cloud of Credit . " What he intends to map out is this invisible web : who is actually putting up the money ? And who is ultimately bearing the risk ? Once it comes out , we can settle these accounts to see which companies are truly bearing the credit risk in this current wave of AI infrastructure expansion . The professor has never doubted that AI will change the world , but life-changing technology and profitable investments are never the same thing . Railroads changed America , but many railroad shareholders and creditors lost their shirts in the process . So , the real question the professor wants to ask is : when the bill finally comes due , who will be left holding the bag ? The core issue discussed in the professor's paper is that the assets only have a six-year shelf life , yet the debt must be repaid over twenty-four years ; buildings remain , chips become obsolete , but the bills must ... eventually ... be paid .
