In early December 1996 , just days after the announcement of Kinder's departure in Houston , Ken Lay booked a private room at the River Oaks Country Club . This place is the most exclusive circle in Houston , where everyone who walks through the door is a local person of status . The guest he invited was someone we’ve mentioned before : Rich Kinder . Who was Kinder ? He was a former Enron executive , the only one who really knew how to manage people , with exceptionally high emotional intelligence that his subordinates respected . He could fire you , and you might even still feel good about it . But Skilling once said , " Rich doesn't understand . " Then , Skilling dispatched his own group of executives to undermine him from behind the scenes . By 1995 , Kinder had left to become the president of a railroad company in Omaha . After 15 months of railroad mergers , he made a graceful exit . A month after he left , a phone call landed on his desk . Lay said , " I want to invite you back . Your seniority and benefits will be fully restored , as if you never left . As for the role ? " Lay added , " Don't be surprised . This has always been Lay's way of recruiting : get the people you like in first , and worry about the position later . " Kinder only wanted one position , and that was Kinder's own . He set a condition , saying , " If I'm to be president , the first thing I'll do is put a leash on those two stars you have . " He was talking about Skilling and Mark . " If you're willing to let me handle them , then we can talk . " Lay didn't take the bait , and the meal ended there . If the story had ended there , there wouldn't be this episode . That same evening , Kinder ran into some old Enron colleagues at a restaurant , had a few drinks , and casually mentioned , " Oh , I had lunch with Ken today . " This remark reached the 31st floor the very next day . Skilling realized immediately that Lay might be trying to bring Kinder back as president . Then , another thought popped into his head : was the woman across the street also vying for this position ? Since Kinder was gone and the position was open , everyone wanted to climb into that seat . She was having a rough time ; her marriage was truly falling apart , and by the following year , they were divorced . She had told colleagues several times , " I'm tired . Sometimes I just want to quit and be done with it . " But no one at Enron took her seriously ; this person could never stop moving . Nine months ago , he just renewed his five-year contract , with his base salary raised to $ 400,000 a year . The contract included a clause that the company had to find him a higher position and title , or pay him $ 1 million before February 1997 . Now the position has opened up , and he knows exactly which one he wants . He went to Ray , using the old tricks from Enron that we’ve discussed : " If this position goes to someone else , I’m leaving . " He added , " If it goes to Rebecca Mark , 70 % of the trading department will walk the next day . " A few days later on a business trip , he told a colleague something even harsher : " If that woman gets the job , I’m out . " Skilling also went to Ray's office to add his own support for Jeff , but what was Ray to do ? He backed down . As I said before , this man hates conflict and never says no . Mark was his favorite , but he couldn't afford to lose Skilling . Look , this guy on the screen is Burns ; the photo is a bit blurry , I know . On December 10 , 1996 , Enron issued a new announcement : Jeff Skilling was named President and COO . That same day , there was news that the Dabhol project was back on track and work in India had resumed . For the two weeks he spent tapping the brakes , he handed the accelerator over to Skilling . Skilling had changed completely over that year . He used to be disheveled , eating junk food all the time ; at the worst point in his marriage , his weight had ballooned to over 200 pounds . A bout of chest pain really scared him , so he started lifting weights and lost over 60 pounds in two months . You can see he has quite the willpower . Later , he started taking diet pills to get his energy back ; that medicine was certainly potent . At 43 , he had never felt more energetic , but as an executive , Skilling hadn't changed a bit . After running ECT for six years , he still kept that consultant mindset , obsessed with " big ideas " and having zero interest in how to actually get things done . He wouldn't even touch the operational nuts and bolts . He couldn't bring himself to manage the people under him , just like Ray couldn't manage his senior executives . In his eyes , success only existed in ECT ; he couldn't be bothered with Enron's other departments . And this is the man tasked with running a multinational corporation with $ 13 billion in revenue , 11,700 employees , operations in 22 countries , and a mountain of messes waiting to be cleaned up . An early ECT executive said , " This is a recipe for disaster . " An absentee boss , a CEO who only makes empty promises and can't manage people . Don't say this is just hindsight ; back then , many people in the company already felt he was in the wrong seat . One person said it to his face : the head of the pipeline company , the one who made the most money , who had run it for ten years — his name was Forrest Hoglund . After the announcement , he went to Skilling , " You're smart , but now that you're CEO , you have nine bosses above you . Sorry , but with your personality , you can't do this job . " Lay never thought about how big Kindler's shoes were , because as the old executive said , Lay didn't know how much Kindler did in a day ; he thought anyone could run the company like Kindler . Blocking Kindler from being CEO was a mistake , and letting Skilling be president was a compounding error . Over the next two years , Skilling transformed Enron into his own image . To Wall Street , he spoke of " an energy machine , " " risk control is the company's lifeblood , " " profits are rising every year , " and " we are a logistics company . " Inside the building , it was an uncontrollable experiment ; one person , two faces , wherever the spotlight hit , people followed — the brighter the light , the darker the shadows . But by the time Skilling took over the mess , Wall Street wasn't in love anymore . Let's first talk about Enron's proudest achievement . From 1989 to 1994 , for six years , Enron met or exceeded profit targets every year ; the stock rose 233 % , while the S & P 500 only rose 65 % — Enron was beating the market . Lay and Kindler bragged about this performance to everyone they met . By 1995 , it broke ; the stock rose 25 % , which was already quite a lot , but the S & P rose 34 % . By 1996 , things became even clearer ; Enron missed its target that year , profits only grew 12 % , three points short of the 15 % goal , underperforming the market for two consecutive years . That wasn't all ; Dabhol collapsed , and by the end of the year , they only recovered by grabbing money from the Arabian Sea coast . The rule on Wall Street is this : when investors love a stock , they forgive everything . Analysts turn a blind eye to problems , believing you when you say a write-off is a one-time event ; they'll even spin the most mundane announcement into something spectacular . But once they turn on you , it’s a whole different story — a relentless , suspicious scrutiny , digging through everything just to unearth the next piece of bad news . They were your sweetheart before , but now you’re just the " Old Woman . " Skilling’s era , unfortunately , happened to coincide with this " Old Woman " phase . Analysts who had given " strong buy " ratings during the Kinder era started downgrading their ratings to " hold " one by one . " Hold , " in Wall Street jargon , actually means " sell . " The female analyst at Prudential , who had been closest to Enron , downgraded her rating in October 1996 . She pointed out a detail : four cents of the quarterly earnings per share came from selling off Teesside shares . The analyst at DLJ downgraded them a few months earlier , for one reason : " Dabhol . " By the spring of 1997 , a Morgan Stanley report stated directly that what concerned everyone most was the complexity of Enron’s business — and how that complexity affected the quality of its earnings . The concept of " earnings quality " was leveled at Enron for the first time . In plain English , it meant : how much of your profit is real , and how much is accounting magic , using various affiliates to conduct related-party transactions ? Investors started looking for the next piece of bad news , and they didn't even have to look — it surfaced on its own : that cabbage contract in the UK , it came back to haunt them . As mentioned before , in March 1993 , when Teesside had the whole of Europe on lockdown and everyone was scrambling for gas , at the very peak of gas prices , Wind was the one who signed the long-term contract . Starting in 1996 , they had to take on a massive amount of North Sea gas every day , nearly as much as Teesside used . That North Sea gas field was called the J-Block , and it was a " take-or-pay " contract — you had to pay regardless of whether you used the gas or not . Ken Lay and Kinder both signed off on it ; what was the plan back then ? Back when Teesside was just starting , everyone thought gas was easy to sell . The person who negotiated that contract was the same West Point roommate of Wind . He even wanted to use that gas to build another Teesside . But all the assumptions were wrong . Enron locked in the price at the market peak , and by 1995 , spot prices had plummeted . Selling this gas meant losing money , and building a power plant was even less profitable . More importantly , in the months after the contract was signed , Enron actually had many chances to offload the gas at cost , but the person who negotiated it moved back to Houston , and the team that took over in London turned away every buyer . They wanted to wait for the market to rise again to make more profit . Later , an executive who wasn’t involved in the contract dug through a drawer and found a one-inch-thick file full of inquiries from companies wanting to buy gas ; Enron had replied to every single one : " Not for sale . " By 1995 , the exposure from this contract neared $ 2 billion . Just think , the entire market cap of Enron was only a little over $ 5 billion . A single contract was enough to cost the company its life . Kinder was furious . One time , he slammed his fist on the conference table and shouted , " Damn it , with all these high-priced geniuses in the room , can't a single one of you tell me what the hell is going on with this contract ? " He started sending team after team from Skilling's office to investigate . One executive read the contract draft and saw that every point of negotiation fell in a way that was disadvantageous to Enron . He even suspected that his own negotiators had taken bribes , so he paid a private investigator out of his own pocket to look into it , but after a full investigation , they found nothing wrong . Kinder flew to the UK himself to talk to Phillips Petroleum and the gas field operators , but the negotiations fell through , and both sides headed to court to sue each other . Enron provided a bunch of technical excuses to try and void the contract , but the court only granted a stay of execution . They didn't have to take the gas until the end of 1997 . At this point , both sides had turned hostile . The Enron London office was sweeping for bugs every day , and the pressure mounted . There was still a $ 1.5 billion hole on the books , but if you look at the documents Enron filed with the SEC , what did they say ? This contract is not expected to have a material adverse effect on the company's financial condition . You might react upon seeing this , " They actually dared to write that ? " Even those in the London office shook their heads when they saw it , and later , someone used the word " inconceivable . " After Kinder left , this region and this entire mess became Skilling's problem . Skilling was not happy at all . What bothered him wasn't how big the hole was , nor was it Wall Street pressuring him to fill it ; it was that he absolutely hated cleaning up other people's messes . Think about it : he is now the president of the entire company , and cleaning up other people's messes is supposed to be the bulk of the job , but he just hated it . You see , Skilling had been a consultant ; he was used to giving advice , but actually having to implement it and fix these skeletons — that’s dirty , grueling work , and he didn't want to do it . In any case , he moved quite quickly . In 1997 , gas prices started to climb , but he wouldn't gamble ; he thought the price was just too precarious . For his first trip as president , he flew to Bartlesville , a small town in Oklahoma where Phillips Petroleum was headquartered . He sat down with the president of Phillips and said , " I want to settle . " The other side agreed . The negotiations dragged on for months and were a headache , but Skilling was willing to pay a high price , which Kinder refused to do — so , what Kinder couldn't accomplish , he did . By June 1997 , Enron paid $ 440 million in cash to Phillips and several other gas field suppliers , and the contracts were changed to market-based pricing , meaning they had to find their own buyers . For this contract alone , Enron had to take a one-time write-off of $ 675 million ; if you're going to purge , you might as well get it all out at once . In the same quarter , Enron also wrote off another $ 100 million for a chemical plant in Houston that produced a gasoline additive called MTBE — another rotten investment he inherited . Combined , those two items wiped out the year’s profits . In his first year as president , Enron only made $ 105 million for the entire year , down over 82 % from the year before . The last time they earned so little was back in 1988 , in those early days when they sold assets just to make ends meet . Of course , this is standard procedure for corporate executives . The play is : when you take office , you clear out all the bad debt from your predecessor in one go so that your future financial reports look better . Skilling later told Fortune magazine a hard truth : " I never want to have another analyst meeting like that second quarter last year , standing in front of a crowd saying we’re wiping out half a billion in profits , with no one in the audience showing a happy face . " I take this matter as a personal grudge of his . Lay and Skilling thought of a way to boost the stock price : share buybacks . When shares are bought back , the number of outstanding shares decreases , and the earnings per share naturally start to look better . In the short term ? Not really . That year , the S & P rose 31 % , while Enron fell 4 % . The annual report explained that this loss was a one-time event , intended to clear away key uncertainties . The opening words of the report , written by them , were " unacceptable , " followed by a promise that things would improve . With the books cleared , he stood behind a clean desk , ready to reshape the entire company in his own image over the next two years . The first thing he did was replace personnel ; people like Pai , Rice , Baxter , and others were all promoted to high-ranking , influential positions at headquarters . In his first year , ECT personnel took 11 of the 26 seats on Enron's management committee , effectively placing his loyalists as heads of various departments . Next , he changed tracks . As we mentioned before , natural gas trading had become difficult as early as 1993 , with competitors opening trading desks , thinning margins , and banks and major clients hiring their own traders . But Skilling couldn't sleep ; his solution was to move beyond natural gas and trade more , with electricity as the next big idea . It was simple : the U.S. wholesale electricity market was worth $ 91 billion annually , three times that of natural gas , and the feds were about to deregulate it ; the first one in would get the biggest slice . He had McKinsey conduct a study , and naturally , McKinsey told him he was right . In his own words , " We've performed this play before , but the situation is a bit different this time . " Back then , Enron was a founding member of the club , but in the electricity market , Enron was an outsider . The national power companies were a tight circle ; while new laws required them to open their transmission lines , they were completely unwilling and threw up roadblocks . When Skilling went to negotiate long-term contracts , they wouldn't even open the door . Skilling came up with a bold move : form an alliance with 12 power companies to build plants , and he sent his people out to pitch it . Not a single one agreed ; all 12 bosses kicked us out of their offices . Skilling later realized they hated us , so if we couldn't get in , we would just buy a ticket . Baxter asked dozens of firms , but no one wanted to deal with Enron until they finally found Portland General Electric in Oregon . Enron offered $ 2.1 billion and assumed $ 1.1 billion in debt , a 46 % premium over the market price . Once the news broke , Enron's own stock price fell by 5 % . This is where Skilling differed from Kinder : if he liked something , price wasn't an issue , believing future profits would cover today's cost . The drama wasn't over ; Enron put on airs before the Oregon regulators , withholding documents and refusing to budge . One commissioner even told Ken Lay to his face , " Don't treat us like a bunch of hicks . " Only when Baxter agreed to cut $ 141 million in electricity rates for Oregonians did the deal finally close in July 1997 . With the ticket in hand , Enron became North America's largest electricity merchant by 1998 , changing its slogan from " the number one natural gas company " to " the world's leading energy company , " even putting the acronym on Skilling’s car . They spent so much on new businesses , but what about the old ones ? Lay had considered selling them off , but never dared to — until he targeted the one area that was Enron's biggest moneymaker . We mentioned the executive , Hoglund , who had been running the oil and gas company since 1987 . It was a separately listed company controlled by Enron , essentially their in-house oil extraction team — a trade Texans had been doing for 100 years . You couldn't find a better-run division at Enron ; output had tripled , and costs were cut to the bone . Whenever Enron’s profits fell short , they would just sell off a chunk of its stock to make up the numbers . In 1994 , the stock price soared , and Hoglund cashed out $ 19 million in options ; Ken Lay used this as a badge of honor — Hoglund made money for shareholders and for himself , lucky guy . But once Skilling took over , this whole setup became an eyesore . Trading was Enron’s real business ; if they wanted more natural gas , they could just buy it on the trading floor — why bother extracting it ? What annoyed him even more was that oil profits fluctuated — high one year , low the next — while Wall Street demanded a smooth , upward line . Hoglund was notoriously tough and never played along with Skilling . Just imagine , right after Skilling took office , Hoglund went straight to him and said , " You aren't fit to be the president of this company . " By the end of 1998 , Occidental Petroleum came knocking , wanting to buy Enron’s controlling interest . Gas prices plummeted , and the oil company's stock dropped to $ 14 — down from $ 25 just a year earlier . Skilling and Baxter signed the deal in a flash ; the company was split in two , with North America going to Occidental and the overseas assets staying with Enron . Occidental Petroleum offered a mix of stock and cash to settle the account , with the cash going to Enron and the stock to the oil and gas company’s shareholders . Hoglund was furious ; the price was too low . The shareholders were getting stock while Enron took the cash — it was clearly a scam against the shareholders . He immediately hired Goldman Sachs as an advisor and demanded a review by independent directors , who ultimately voted the deal down . After five months of dragging things out , Hoglund managed to kill the deal . Now it was Skilling’s turn to be angry . He sent Baxter to talk directly to Hoglund . After weeks of negotiation , Hoglund won . The oil and gas company became independent . Enron sold its shares back to them for $ 600 million in cash plus their assets in China and India , valued at $ 22 per share . After the deal was done , Baxter sent a card with flowers : " Hoglund , we finally made the right deal . " Hoglund replied in a letter : " Dear Cliff , the right deal was made , but you must understand , I'm from the Midwest ; accepting flowers means we're engaged . " Afterward , Hoglund retired . By the end of 2000 , the oil and gas company’s stock had risen to $ 54 , more than double Enron’s selling price . Skilling was in such a rush to dump it that he threw away $ 1.1 billion . So you see , of those who dared to say no to Skilling , Hoglund was the only one who won . He won , and what’s more , he walked away with a fortune . With the troublemaker Hoglund gone , another troublemaker , Rebecca Mark , remained at the company . The day Skilling became president , Mark was livid . Ken Lay had lied to her ; he had personally told her there would be no personnel changes in the short term . For the next two years , the two played cat and mouse . When they were peers , Mark could block Skilling , but now , Skilling had transformed into her boss . She knew deep down that her days at Enron were numbered . She played no part in this battle from start to finish , and the media later dubbed it " asset versus asset . " Mark herself said , " Jeff hates anything hard , meaning these hard assets . " That’s not true ; the pipelines gave him the secret to gas trading . Portland General gave him a ticket to power trading ; later , he bought these businesses to trade pulp , and bought fiber optics to trade bandwidth . He had a formula : buy hard assets , crack the code of a business , master all the skills , and then dump the assets . Over these past few years , Enron has actually spent a lot of money in India — likely at least several billion . What Skilling truly despised was that the deals Mark signed lacked any clear strategy ; she’d go to any country willing to take them , building any kind of power plant . In the words of a former executive , she took whatever deal she could find , spreading resources thin all over the world . Mark loved to talk about bringing light to poor countries , but that executive retorted : supplying power to places in the dark is charity , not business . Skilling couldn't be sure , though ; this was a different beast . Mark’s deals never went through internal company audits , and you could never even get a budget figure out of her . Another issue was money ; Mark’s team got a bonus for every contract signed , regardless of whether the project would actually be profitable later . For some small projects , she took $ 2 million for herself — which is fair , but a bit hypocritical ; didn't ECT traders also get paid the day they signed a deal ? Skilling did the same thing , so while he was angry , fighting Mark wasn't easy . His project , the first big move after becoming CEO , was to ask Ken Lay for resources , and Enron could serve as the foundation for the European trading desk . Besides , didn't those people building power plants need ECT to manage their risks ? Hadn't the lessons from the Dabhol project been painful enough ? Mark went to Lay to plead her case , and this battle had already reached a breaking point at a meeting in San Antonio . Mark claimed that her people understood Europe the best . Skilling argued that this was obvious — we build something , and you just snatch it away — but Ken Lay sided with Skilling . Mark took her deputy , a military veteran , stood up , and walked out on him . Later , recounting what Ken Lay said afterward , Lay remarked , " I've been in business my whole life , and no one has ever walked out on me . " Mark replied , " Ken , everything you want , is all just what Jeff wants . " From the outside , Mark was still riding high ; Fortune magazine had just named her one of the 50 most powerful women in American business , reporting she was a potential successor to Lay , but she knew she had already lost the war . She wanted to get as far away from Skilling as possible , and she hit on an idea : sell the entirety of Enron International to a competitor . Skilling agreed to negotiate the terms , with Baxter leading the talks — this was his own brainchild . After months of negotiation , the price tag hovered around half the shares , at most 3 billion dollars , but the suitor never made a formal offer , fearing the two companies wouldn't mesh . Mark blamed Baxter and Skilling for botching the negotiations . The previous summer , Mark invited Skilling to South America for a week to see his projects , and Skilling agreed . He had used this tactic before on board members : wining and dining them abroad , showing them construction sites until they were satisfied , but guess what happened ? This trick worked on Skilling ; he came back wanting to pour money into South America , using the same old excuse : buying infrastructure would spark a whole new line of trading business . To what extent was he excited ? He partnered with a female executive from the South American division to buy a beach house in Brazil , and rumors in the company spread instantly , saying he was infatuated with that charismatic woman . The rumors had two implications , neither of which sounded good , and both denied them . But rumors were just rumors ; Skilling continued to make life miserable for Mark , piece by piece dismantling Enron International's independence . In May 1998 , Mark had had enough ; he handed the Enron International CEO position to that female deputy , keeping only the title of Chairman , plus another title : Vice Chairman of Enron . People in the company called this title the " ejection seat " — once you sit in it , it’s the first step toward being kicked out the door . If you look back at the accounts from those two years , one thing really stands out . For two years , Mark kept signing deals and building power plants , and while Skilling talked about how rotten Mark's assets were and how unbelievable his reported numbers were , the money never stopped for a day , and Mark’s deals never stopped either . Skilling later explained to friends that Mark had Lay's protection and the board's support , so he simply couldn't control him . There were a few deals I should have thrown myself under a truck to stop , but what would that have accomplished ? You see , it’s clear Skilling wanted to dump the blame for Enron’s bankruptcy on Mark , but many of those projects of Mark’s were signed off by Skilling himself . When Mark decided to step down , the company gave him a new contract with a base salary of 710,000 dollars , plus stock options . In 1998 and 1999 , the company even forgave 1.6 million dollars in loans for him . Mark is Mark . Even after stepping to the sidelines , he didn't stay there long . One month into his role as Vice Chairman , he proposed a plan to the board for Enron to enter a business they had never considered : water . Back then , Mark wanted to acquire a British water utility , Wessex Water , for $ 2.4 billion . The water business is actually quite treacherous . Two French giants dominated the profits , relying on meticulous cost-cutting and operations , which wasn't Enron's style at all . Think about it : their president came from a consulting background and had no interest in doing such dirty , laborious work . But Mark’s pitch was compelling — that the world would soon face a water shortage . The board members were undecided , so they turned to another director , Skilling , and asked , " What do you think ? " He calmly replied , " If this actually works , it’s a good business . " Of course , he left out the second half : if they approved this deal , Mark would never be in his way again . The price was high ; he had calculated it himself , and Mark was overpaying by $ 200 million . But that didn't matter , as Mark would have to shoulder the subsequent financial burdens himself . Whether it succeeded or not ... ... was his own concern . So , the deal was worth doing , regardless of whether it made sense for the company . Just think : the reason Skilling gave his nod to a $ 2.4 billion acquisition was simply to drive Mark away so he wouldn't be a bother to him at the company anymore . Even with his approval , the board vote wasn't unanimous ; two directors voted against it . It was the only time the Enron board hadn't voted unanimously since their inception , but the motion passed , and Mark was back in the game . The new company was called Azurix , a name coined by brand consultants . A few years later , it was this very company that cracked the first visible hole in Enron’s door . That same year , Skilling went around Wall Street talking about one thing : Enron's risk management . When reporters and analysts asked about it , he had a ready-made answer : Enron had a dedicated Risk Assessment and Control department . In 1996 , he elevated it from being under the CEO to a company-wide department . He knew what Wall Street wanted to see ; every time they reported to analysts and rating agencies , this department was always the star of the presentation . He told a financial magazine that there were two things at Enron that were non-negotiable : the performance review system and company-wide risk management . On paper , at least , this department certainly looked impressive . Every transaction over $ 500,000 had to go through a risk assessment . With 150 experts , a $ 30 million annual budget , and a $ 600 million computer system , they had a formal document called a deal approval sheet , signed by everyone required . The form even had a dedicated box to write " not recommended , " managed by a man named Rick Buy , who joined ECT from Bankers Trust with Skilling in 1994 . He became Chief Risk Officer in 1998 , and Skilling called him Enron’s police chief . People said he reported directly to the board’s audit committee , but if Buy felt a deal was too simple , Enron wouldn't do it . Wall Street was completely fooled ; an S & P analyst told Fortune , " We rely heavily on Enron's risk management ; it gives us a warm , fuzzy feeling . " What did Buy himself say in an internal Enron promotional video ? " We move fast here , the kitchen is hot , and we don't want anyone who drags their feet . " How did people inside Enron actually view this risk management department ? In the words of a former managing director : " Risk management is a speed bump , not a roadblock . As long as a deal sounds plausible , it can get done . I treat them like crap , and there’s nothing they can do . " Why was this department so weak ? Buy , round-faced and bespectacled , spoke softly and hid whenever a real argument broke out . When his subordinates raised questions , he would just pass them up ; he never dared to face the dealmakers head-on . According to a veteran dealmaker , the risk department was just for the analysts , meant to keep the story going . Buy was a nice guy , but he wasn't tough enough . In 1998 , ECT Europe wanted to invest $ 20 million to buy a British cabinet fiberboard factory that was already in bankruptcy proceedings . One of his deputies said , " Don't sign anything . " Buy signed it anyway ; three months later the factory was liquidated , and the $ 20 million went to zero . It gets worse : the dealmakers had the authority to change the risk department’s comments on the approval sheet . They would sit at the same table , tweak the wording , and deals worth hundreds of millions would be reviewed in days — the company just needed him to rubber-stamp them . When it came to performance reviews , the dealmakers graded the risk staff . A risk VP said if you upset a dealmaker , they could drop you from tier one to tier four , a six-figure difference in bonuses , which was truly absurd . Skilling himself was struggling , too . At a lunch in 1999 , he complained to a trader . " I feel like I don't count for anything . " The other person said , " Then why don't you quit ? " He replied , " I have to stay , the money is just too good . " By 2001 , he had sold $ 4.3 million worth of Enron stock . The pay was indeed very high . Did Skilling know he was soft ? Of course he did . When he left Enron , he advised Lay to hire someone tougher , but why didn't he replace him himself ? He said he was watching closely . But how did he watch ? During meetings , he would shout , " I don't like this deal , I hate this deal , " and then turn to his trusted trader and say , " If you really do it , this is your imperial sword , but if something goes wrong , you're on your own . " A risk management executive shook his head slowly after recounting this scene . Too many imperial swords were handed out . After Enron collapsed , he defended this system , claiming that those in charge hadn't used their power well . One person didn't see it that way . Amanda Martin , a female managing director at ECT who managed those traders , said it was no coincidence that a soft person was chosen as the risk officer . If one only said , " I won't sign this , I'm going to the board . " Skilling would back down , and the deal would be dead . Since they didn't say it , Skilling could always claim , " I have always respected risk management . " Skilling was playing a game of who would blink first , but no one ever stood up to him until the end . Just like with the risk management at Enron , Skilling handled everything else with just these superficial gestures . He said , " The company must tolerate failure . I want a smart loser , not a lucky winner . " But when Enron botched a deal , basically no one took responsibility . By the time the deal fell apart , the traders had long since collected their bonuses and changed positions . He also said that companies obsessed with costs couldn't produce good ideas . " We aren't the Walmart of the natural gas industry ; we are Mercedes-Benz . " A managing director put it more bluntly : " If you're watching costs , you've already failed . " So , what was Enron like back then ? If you wanted a top-of-the-line computer , it would be on your desk in hours . On many floors , people were ordering takeout on the company's dime every day . Secretaries each had a company phone , and if anyone had a half-baked idea to do business in Europe , they'd buy a ticket and fly to London , first class , staying in luxury hotels — no one cared at all . A veteran marketing vice president said , " As long as the profit targets are met , expenses don't matter . " bonuses were paid out just the same . Some estimated that Enron’s global administrative expenses were . $ 1.8 billion a year , with consulting fees alone at $ 750 million . It wasn't until 2000 that the company appointed a procurement czar , centralizing management , and travel expenses immediately . dropped by a quarter , and then there was the moving . By 1998 , a dedicated moving crew worked every night ; just shifting workstations cost $ 6 million a year , with some employees moving three or four times . Departments were split and merged constantly ; the HR department , receiving the new org chart , would just stamp a date on it and call it " today's reorganization . " Where were the people heading ? Skilling had a theory that employees voted with their feet , and that businesses with more people were better businesses , but where did people go ? It depended on what he said on stage ; a former vice president named it the " Spotlight Effect . " Wherever Jeff Skilling stood , talent would flock , and the work would follow the light . In the words of another former VP , Enron was just one never-ending job hunt . Looking back , the ECT folks say the place had magic in the early years , but by the late 90s , money was the only thing that mattered . Those who valued teamwork were pushed out , leaving behind the cutthroat , the ones best at padding their own pockets , even stealing from their own internal departments — that was considered " creative . " As for the clients , Martin said , we offended every single major power customer we had . The word on the street was : don't do business with Enron , one moment of carelessness and your wallet’s gone ; they were practically thugs . You can see that Skilling’s management skills were truly mediocre , and the entire company culture was deeply flawed . One year , Skilling took a group of executives and clients , all men , to Mexico for a business trip , a 1,200 - mile off-road race with Jeeps and motorcycles . Rice later remarked that Jeff Skilling’s criteria for choosing locations could actually get people killed . This trip nearly did kill someone ; Rice slipped off his motorcycle . A tooth pierced his lip , a Jeep flipped completely over , and another companion broke several ribs . The first to arrive was Fastow , who rolled off a ridge himself and landed right on a cactus . Those arriving later didn't see paramedics , but rather a group of people pulling cactus needles out of Fastow . The finish line was a rented mansion called the Golden Crest Villa . The chefs had prepared a feast , and a row of massage therapists was waiting . Chartered flights back to Houston , with a photo album for everyone . These employees truly entered the Enron legend , living a life of high-stakes luxury with an entire fleet of company jets , limousines on call , and butlers to do your dry cleaning . In the days when bonuses were handed out , Houston's luxury car dealerships had lines for Porsches . Because as the old executives said , the money had rotted their brains . " When I get off the company jet and take a limo home , I have to remind myself that I'm a normal person . " If you live like this for too long without strong moral principles , you lose them quickly ; no one proved this more true than Rice . He was a kid from a Nebraska farm who became a multimillionaire in his 30s , obsessed with fast cars and motorcycles . In 1996 , while married , he began an affair with Martin that lasted three years ; the whole company knew , even though Martin was his subordinate . That same year , Martin received an anonymous envelope containing the salary information of a male colleague promoted to managing director alongside her ; with identical evaluations , the man's base salary was $ 300,000 , while hers was only $ 225,000 . Her bonus had been over $ 100,000 less than his for two consecutive years ; she took the envelope to Skilling , and two weeks later , she received a check for $ 300,000 . In her own words , Enron was a tough place for women ; once the relationship went public , the gossip started that she only advanced because she slept with the boss . Someone reported this to the board , and after the two confessed , Skilling's solution was to move Martin to a water company in the UK , while Rice faced no consequences at all . Skilling himself got involved with a risk management manager from the trading floor ; insiders say they were together before reporting it to the board , after which Skilling promoted her to corporate secretary , managing board meeting minutes , and by the time she left , her salary was nearly $ 600,000 ; just imagine paying a corporate secretary that much . The company , thoroughly transformed by Skilling , cared about only one thing : stock price ; there was a stock ticker in the lobby , CNBC in the elevators , and employees were constantly urged to buy Enron stock , with more than half of their pension accounts held in Enron shares . In 1998 , the stock price reached $ 50 . Ken Lay and Skilling saw it as a grand celebration , handing every employee a $ 50 bill , because a former assistant said the stock price was Jeff Skilling's report card ; when traveling , he'd call several times a day just to ask , " What is it at ? " You can see that back then , checking stock prices wasn't as convenient as it is now . In 1998 , Wall Street had a change of heart . The S & P rose 27 % that year , while Enron surged 37 % . Analysts all switched back to " buy , " and with profits growing over 15 % annually , hitting targets looked very easy . Just think about it : by 1999 , this company had 18,000 employees . A former executive estimated that 6,000 were working on deals , with 5,000 being traders and deal-makers . Skilling himself said it quite bluntly : " We are just resellers . " But this statement could not be uttered to the outside world , not even a word . In December 1995 , a rumor spread on Wall Street that Enron had gone short on natural gas and hit a cold snap , losing a fortune , and Skilling had people escorted out of the trading floor . This was false , but the market truly believed it ! In a single day , the stock price evaporated by $ 750 million . The three bosses held an emergency conference call overnight , with 170 institutions listening to them claim Enron’s risk control was airtight . The more fatal issue is actually a simple truth : a company that relies on trading for a living cannot have stable annual growth . A trading desk can make tens of millions in the blink of an eye , or lose tens of millions just as fast . Goldman Sachs is the best at trading in the world , yet their P / E ratio rarely exceeds 20 times . They never forecast their profits because it’s impossible to predict . Enron’s valuation was double that of Goldman’s . Skilling wasn't satisfied ; he told the public , " We are a logistics company , simply moving electricity from power plants to customers ' hands . " " Our trading desks are delta-neutral , meaning we have no long or short directional bets ; we only collect fees and don't gamble on the direction . " This claim was repeated until the day they closed their doors , even though it hadn't been true for a long time . So how were the profit targets set ? In the old days , they would calculate department by department to arrive at a figure , but Skilling would ask the analysts directly , " What profit do you need to see to support our stock price ? " Whatever number the analysts wanted ... ... he would push that number down to the company . Could the company actually achieve it ? It didn't matter . Because as an executive said , the budget was a massive game of who would be the first to blink . If you wanted to say , " Wait , I can't do that , " and you actually said it , you would be the one out of luck . So , at the end of every quarter , Enron would go into a frenzy . As the quarter neared its end , the higher-ups would realize they couldn't meet the heavy targets set by Wall Street . For any other company , this would be the time for the CEO to step out and issue a warning that this quarter might fall slightly short . But not Enron . The reality at Enron , from start to finish , was focused on only one thing : hitting those numbers at any cost . For that , the company had a specific department called the " crying room , " where the CFO and the chief accountant would make calls to heads of various business units : " Get me another $ 15 million . " " How are you supposed to pull that out of thin air ? " They’d pressure colleagues to rush negotiations , arguing that spending a few extra millions in the long run didn't matter — just get through this quarter first . Mark-to-market accounting — we've talked about that method before — was originally approved only for natural gas contracts . Over the years , it quietly crept into power trading ; in 1996 it spread to bandwidth , and by 1997 it took over the entire trading business , even influencing risk management . The value of those things was essentially just a wild guess . By the turn of the century , more than a third of Enron's assets were valued this way . Traders would look back at five-year-old contracts , see if they could squeeze a few more millions out of them , tweak them , or reinterpret them to show an extra profit . That contract library was called the " gold mine . " They’d project price curves 20 years out — which was just a guess anyway — so they made it an optimistic one . By nudging the curves up , millions in profit would appear . If the books didn't match up later , it didn't matter ; that was a problem for the future . They had another trick called " kicking the can down the road . " When it came time to write off dead deals at quarter-end , the CFO would go department by department . Once , an executive saw the CFO fixated on a dead deal and asked , " Is this deal really beyond saving ? " The executive replied , " It's gone . " The CFO asked , " Is there absolutely no chance of it coming back ? " " None . " The CFO said , " Not even the slightest chance ? Why don't you take another look ? " The executive understood ; the deal was suddenly declared " back from the dead , " pushing the bad debt into the next quarter . He later said that the first time you do it , it feels rotten , but after a while , it doesn't feel so bad anymore . You just get used to it . And there were taxes . Starting in 1995 , the 250 - person tax department executed 11 complex transactions , conjuring up $ 510 million in profit . But after Skilling became CEO , the company increasingly treated the tax department as a profit center . The head of the department wrote in an email , " We've created a profit-generating business segment for Enron . " These tricks all shared a common effect : pushing problems further into the future , layer by layer , growing narrower and thicker . When they couldn't push them any further , they turned to the " piggy bank . " In 1996 , Enron spent 185 million dollars to acquire a private company that made deep-sea drilling equipment . Private companies have no market price , and deep-sea exploration is a gamble — so how much is it really worth ? It's unclear , and Enron relied on that ambiguity . Whenever profits fell short , they would write up the valuation . By the second quarter of 2001 , Enron's books valued it at 367 million dollars . What did Enron's own risk management team calculate it to be ? Between 47 million and 196 million . Risk management brought it up every year , but when they sat on the board , they didn't push back at all . After bankruptcy and liquidation , 257 million was wiped out . There was also a group of about 20 people specializing in buying equity outside the energy sector . A steel mill in Thailand , a paper mill in Canada , and a steel mill in the U.S. Every Monday morning , risk management would meet to review the portfolio : green lights , yellow lights , red lights . This group had more and more red lights , and the head of the group was promoted to managing director . In 1999 , risk management went over the books and found a loss of over 400 million dollars . Skilling said , " We know oil , but we don't know Thai steel . " The tuition for this lesson was quite expensive . For 400 million dollars , even for Enron , this hole wasn't easy to fill . How could they fill it without affecting reported profits ? Deep within Enron , there was another team working on exactly that . The person leading the team was someone we saw earlier with the cactus — the guy who had people pulling cactus needles out of his backside . So you see , from that point on , the company had already developed all sorts of bad habits , specifically maintaining high profit growth through creative accounting . The recipe for disaster is never just one bad person controlling a company , but a room full of smart people and a culture where no one is willing to speak up .
