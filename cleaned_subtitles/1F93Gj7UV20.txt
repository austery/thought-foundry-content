In this video, we're explaining 10 pricing psychology tricks, from nine endings to missing dollar signs. You'll probably recognize a few as we go. Let's start with number one. Why $9.99 instead of $10? Because that first nine can pull your impression toward nine, even when you know it's almost 10. The saving is 1 cent. The impression can be much bigger. And price endings can do something else, too. A nine can signal a bargain, even when the price isn't the lowest. In one experiment, a clothing company sent out different versions of the same catalog. The same dress was priced at $34 in some, 39 in others, and 44 in the rest. The $39 dress outsold both. It sold more than the cheaper version. That study tested dollar prices ending in nine, not a 1 cent discount. But it shows why the ending deserves attention, and it's only trick number one. Here's the thing most people miss. You don't actually know what most things should cost. How much should a coffee be? A gym membership? A new phone? You have a rough idea, but no exact number. So, your brain looks for clues. The number next to the price, the other options on the menu, the way the price is written, whether you pay with cash or a card. Stores know this, and every one of those clues can be designed. We'll go through all 10 in three groups: tricks on the price tag, tricks in the choices you're given, and tricks in how you pay. And at the end, a few habits to help you shop around them. Let's start with the price tag itself. Why does $9.99 work? One explanation is that we read from left to right, and can give too much weight to the first digit. So, $9.99 can feel more like nine something than 10. Researchers call it the left digit effect. It matters most when that first digit changes. $2.99 to $3.00 can feel like a bigger jump than that 1 cent deserves. A change within the same leading digit does not cross that boundary. It even shows up in used cars, where prices drop noticeably every time the mileage crosses a round number, like 59,900 to 60,000 mi, even though the car is basically the same. The nine has a second effect, too. Over the years, we've learned that prices ending in nine usually mean a sale or a bargain. So, the ending itself says cheap, even when it isn't. Was 120, now 60. Is 60 a good price for that shirt? You have no idea. But, it feels like a steal because your brain is comparing it to 120. This is anchoring. The first number you see becomes the yardstick for everything after it. Even when that number is meaningless. How meaningless? In a famous 1970s experiment, psychologists spun a wheel of fortune in front of people. It was rigged to land on either 10 or 65. Then, they asked, "What percentage of United Nations member countries are African?" The median guess was 25% in the group shown 10, and 45% in the group shown 65. A random number from a wheel moved their answer by 20 points. Now, think about the original price on a sale tag. Some stores barely sell anything at that price. It's main job is to be the anchor. You'll also see anchors on menus and websites. A $400 bottle of wine at the top of the list can make the $80 bottle feel reasonable, whether or not anyone buys the expensive one. The enterprise plan on a software site at 500 a month makes the $50 plan look like nothing. Compare a watch priced at 2,000 with a bargain marked 1997. Round prices can fit a clean, premium presentation. Odd prices can suggest someone squeezed out every cent. But, the effect depends on the product and the situation. A round number is not proof of quality. So, businesses pick the ending based on what they want you to feel. A budget airline wants you to feel you're getting a deal. A designer brand wants you to feel the price isn't the point. There's a twist on this for big purchases. Very precise prices, like a house listed at $494,800 instead of $500,000, can make the seller look like they've calculated exactly what it's worth. Some experiments find that people adjust less from a precise starting price. Precise can sound researched. Round can sound more negotiable. That is a tendency, not a valuation of the house. Small coffee, $3. Large, seven. Which do you pick? Imagine you were going to pick the small. Now add a medium at 650. Suddenly the large looks amazing. It's only 50 cents more than the medium. You might pass over the medium and choose the large. The medium has given you a new comparison, making the large look like a bargain. The most famous example comes from a subscription offer for The Economist magazine, studied by the behavioral economist Dan Ariely. There were three options: online only, $59; print only, $125; print and online, $125. Who would ever choose print only when print and online cost the same? Nobody. In the classroom demonstration Ariely reported, 84% picked print and online. Then he removed the useless print only option. Same prices otherwise. Now most students picked the cheap online only plan, and only 32% picked print and online. An option nobody chose more than doubled the share choosing the expensive plan in that demonstration. Those were students' choices, not measured magazine sales. When we're unsure, we avoid extremes. The cheapest option feels like it might be bad. The most expensive feels like a rip-off. The middle one feels safe. This is called the compromise effect, and businesses use it on purpose. Want to sell more of your $300 product? Add a $500 version above it. The $300 one is now the sensible middle choice, and it may gain appeal even if few people choose the $500 one. Next time you see three plans labeled basic, pro, and premium, with pro marked most popular, ask yourself, is it the best for me or just the one in the middle? In one experiment, researchers offered people a choice. A fancy Lindt truffle for 15 cents, or a Hershey's Kiss for 1 cent. Among those choosing a chocolate, most chose the truffle. Good chocolate, good deal. Then they cut both prices by 1 cent. Truffle 14 cents, Kiss free. The price gap was exactly the same, but now most people chose the free Kiss. Free can feel different from a very low price. In these experiments, the researchers found evidence that the positive feeling of getting something free helped explain the shift. That's why free shipping over 35 does makes you add things to your cart, and why buy one get one free feels better than 50% off two. Often the maths is identical. Just $1 a day. Charities, gyms, and streaming services love this line. $365 a year sounds like a real decision. $1 a day sounds like nothing. You compare it to a coffee or a snack. Small things you buy without thinking. Researchers call this temporal reframing, and studies found it makes people more willing to give and to pay. It's the same amount of money. It's just been cut into pieces small enough to swallow. The reverse works, too. If a company wants a saving to look big, they show it as a yearly total. Save $120 a year sounds far better than save $10 a month. Handing over a $50 note feels like something. Tapping a card feels like nothing. Economists call that feeling the pain of paying, and it's real. In a well-known study at MIT, students bid on tickets to a sold-out Boston Celtics game. Half were told they'd have to pay in cash. The other half were told they could pay by credit card. The card group bid roughly twice as much. Same tickets, but different randomly assigned groups of students. The payment instructions changed. This is why stores love cards. One-click buying and saved payment details. Reducing the friction of paying can encourage spending, although the effect varies across people and purchases. At many casino tables, you exchange dollars for chips. Arcades give you tokens. Mobile games make you buy gems, then spend the gems. Theme parks sell points. Each one puts a step between you and your actual money. Spending 100 gems doesn't feel like spending $4.99, especially when the gems come in packs that never quite match the price of what you want, so there's always some left over pulling you back. Have you noticed that fancy restaurants often write prices like 18 instead of $18.00? That's on purpose. A study at Cornell's hotel school gave diners different menus. Some with dollar signs, some with prices written out in words, and some with plain numbers. At that one restaurant, diners given plain numbers spent more on average than diners whose menus included monetary cues, such as the dollar sign or the word dollars. One possible explanation was that monetary cues reminded diners of the pain of paying. It does not mean removing a dollar sign will raise spending in every restaurant. Strip out the dollar sign and the price becomes just a number on a nice piece of paper. Restaurants have other tricks, too. They put the price at the end of the description instead of in a neat column on the right, so you can't easily scan down and pick the cheapest dish. And the most profitable dishes often sit where your eyes land first, in a box or at the top of a section. That's 10 tricks. The nine at the end, the anchor, round versus odd, the decoy, the middle option, free, pennies a day, cards, chips, and the missing dollar sign. So, how do you shop without falling for all of them? You won't become immune to these. Knowing the names does not make you immune, but a few habits can help you slow down and compare the actual costs. One, round up always. $9.99 is $10. $49.95 is $50. Read the price you'll actually pay, not the first digit. Two, ignore the was price. Ask one question instead. Would I pay this price if there were no other number next to it? Three, find the decoy. When you see three options, look for the one that seems pointless. It may be making its neighbor look better. Four, pick your option before you see the menu. Decide what you need first, then check which plan or size matches it, not the other way around. Five, convert it back to real money. A dollar a day is $365 a year. A thousand gems is however many dollars you paid for them. Free shipping over $35 isn't free if you spend 20 more to get it. Six, make paying feel real again. For things you tend to overspend on, try paying with a debit card or even cash and turn off one-click buying. So, back to the question from the start. Why not $10? Because a price isn't just a number, it's a message designed by people who've studied exactly how your brain reads it. The nine says bargain. The anchor says deal. The decoy says smart choice. The card says don't worry about it. None of these tricks force you to buy anything. They just nudge. But nudges add up, purchase after purchase, year after year. The good news is, once you've seen them, it's very hard to unsee them. Next time you're in a store, count how many you can spot. And if you see a price that ends in 99, you'll know exactly what it's trying to say.
